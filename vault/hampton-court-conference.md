The Hampton Court Conference was the meeting in January 1604 between [[james-i|James I]], his bishops, and four puritan ministers, called in answer to the [[millenary-petition]]. The Muse stops here because this is where the [[king-james-bible]] was first proposed. A puritan, [[john-rainolds|John Rainolds]], asked for a new translation. The Bishop of London, [[richard-bancroft|Richard Bancroft]], objected. James took the idea up anyway, said the Geneva was the worst English Bible he knew, and ruled out marginal notes, because he had found notes in the Geneva he called "seditious."

## Historical Context

James came to the English throne in 1603. The puritan ministers' petition was presented to him "on his way to London after his accession," and the editors of the standard 1896 collection date it April 1603 (Gee & Hardy 1896, headnote). The petition itself offered to settle its complaints "by conference among the learned to be resolved" (Gee & Hardy 1896). Roger Lockyer, in the *Oxford Companion to British History*, puts the link plainly: "James responded by summoning the Hampton Court conference and using the millenary petition as its agenda."

Barlow's printed account dates the conference "Ianuary 14. 1603" on its title page (Barlow 1604). That is Old Style dating, when the year began on 25 March, so it means January 1604. An encyclopedia article on the conference hosted by the Christian Classics Ethereal Library gives the meeting days: "The conference met on Jan. 14, 16, and 18." Barlow's text confirms the 14th and the 16th.

Frederick Shriver, revisiting the conference in 1982, put its weight this way: "The conference between James I, some of the bishops and representatives of the puritans at Hampton Court palace in January 1604 was one of the most significant events in the political and religious history of England" (Shriver 1982, first page).

## The Idea

Almost everything we know about what was said comes from one book, William Barlow's *[[summe-and-substance-of-the-conference|The Summe and Substance of the Conference]]*, printed in London in 1604. The quotations below are Barlow's record, in the spelling of the 1604 print (EEBO-TCP A04434). They are what he printed, not a transcript.

### The puritans come in

The puritan side was admitted on the second day: "ON Munday, Ianuary, 16. betweene 11. and 12. of the Clocke, were the foure Plaintiffes called into the Priuie Chamber." The four were Reynolds (Rainolds), Sparkes, Knewstubs, and Chaderton, and Barlow calls Rainolds "D. Reynalds the Foreman" (Barlow 1604, second day).

### The proposal

Rainolds asked for a new Bible:

> "After that, he moued his Maiestie, that there might bee a newe translation of the Bible, because, those which were allowed in the raignes of Henrie the eight, and Edward the sixt, were corrupt and not aunswerable to the truth of the Originall." (Barlow 1604)

He gave three examples of bad renderings, from Gal. 4:25, Ps. 105:28, and Ps. 106:30. On the second: "Secondly, Psalme, 105. 28. they were not obedient; The Originall beeing, They were not disobedient." Barlow brushed them off as "triuiall and old, and alreadie, in print, often aunswerred" (Barlow 1604).

Bancroft pushed back: "onely, my Lord of London well added, that if euery mans humour should be followed, there would be no ende of translating" (Barlow 1604). The "Lord of London" was Richard Bancroft, elected Bishop of London in 1597 (DNB, "Bancroft, Richard").

### James's answer

> "VVhereupon his Highnesse wished, that some especiall paines should be taken in that behalfe for one vniforme translation (professing that hee could neuer, yet, see a Bible well translated in English; but the worst of all, his Maiestie thought the Geneua to bee)" (Barlow 1604)

He laid out who would do it: "and this to bee done by the best learned in both the Vniuersities, after them to bee reuiewed by the Bishops, and the chiefe learned of the Church; from them to bee presented to the Priuie-Councell; and lastly to bee ratified by his Royall authoritie; and so this whole Church to be bound vnto it, and none other" (Barlow 1604).

### No notes

Then came the rule the finished Bible is best known for, and it was Bancroft who prompted it:

> "Marry, withall, hee gaue this caueat (vpon a word cast out by my Lord of London) that no marginall notes should be added" (Barlow 1604)

James gave his reason. He had seen the notes of the [[geneva-bible|Geneva Bible]]:

> "hauing found in them, which are annexed to the Geneua translation (which he sawe in a Bible giuen him by an English Lady) some notes very partiall, vntrue, seditious, and sauouring too much, of daungerous, and trayterous conceites" (Barlow 1604)

And he named two:

> "As for example, Exod. 1. 19. where the marginall note alloweth disobedience to Kings. And 2. Chron. 15. 16. the note taxeth Asa for deposing his mother, onely, and not killing her" (Barlow 1604)

Barlow doesn't say which edition of the Geneva the English lady gave him, so this page doesn't either. The 1804 reprint of Barlow gives the same passage in modernized spelling, which is a second copy of the text, not a second witness to the event.

### What came of it

The encyclopedia article on CCEL says "Reynolds' request for anew translation of the Bible bore fruit in the so-called Authorized Version" (as printed). The ban on notes carried into the translators' rules. Rule 6, in the text Burnet printed and Pollard reprinted in 1911, reads: "No Marginal Notes at all to be affixed, but only for the Explanation of the Hebrew or Greek Words, which cannot without some circumlocution, so briefly and fitly be express'd in the Text." The English delegates to the Synod of Dort, reporting on the work in 1618, said the same: "Secundo, ut nullae annotationes margini apponerentur : sed tantum loca parallela notarentur" (Ward, in the *Acta* of 1620, as printed by Westcott 1905).

The translators themselves told the story less kindly to the puritans. Their 1611 preface, [[translators-to-the-reader|The Translators to the Reader]], says the conference was called on puritan petitions, and that the puritans brought up the translation when their other grounds failed. In its words: "the very Historicall trueth is, that upon the importunate petitions of the Puritanes, at this Majesties comming to this Crowne, the Conference at Hampton Court having bene appointed for hearing their complaints" ... "a most corrupted translation." ... "yet even hereupon did his Majestie beginne to bethinke himselfe of the good that might ensue by a new translation" (Wikisource transcription of the 1611 text).

## Claims & Disputes

- **Established:** James said the Geneva notes were seditious. Barlow prints him calling them "very partiall, vntrue, seditious," and gives his two examples, Exod. 1:19 and 2 Chron. 15:16 (Barlow 1604).
- **Contested:** whether the Geneva notes really were seditious. Tom Furniss (2009) records the long-standing view: "Since its first publication in 1560, the Geneva Bible has been considered by many as a revolutionary or seditious text, especially because of the numerous explanatory notes that the translators added in the margins of the text." His own position is that "its politics are undecidable" ... "While some of the Geneva notes and prefaces encourage a revolutionary response to tyrants, there are many others that recommend obedience or passive resistance" (Furniss 2009, abstract). So this page says James *called* the notes seditious. It doesn't say they were.
- **Analysis, not a finding:** why the notes would have bothered James in particular. Six years earlier, in *[[trew-law-of-free-monarchies|The Trew Law of Free Monarchies]]* (1598), he had written that "a wicked king is sent by God for a curse to his people, and a plague for their sinnes: but that it is lawfull to them to shake off that curse at their owne hand, which God hath laid on them, that I deny" (McIlwain 1918). A note that "alloweth disobedience to Kings" cuts straight against that. The connection is this page's reading of the two texts side by side. No source quoted here makes it.
- **Barlow is not neutral.** His own asides show it: Bancroft "well added," Rainolds's examples were "triuiall and old." Read the quotations as Barlow's account of the day.
- **Contested (framing):** Lockyer describes James using the petition "as its agenda." How closely the conference actually followed the petition is a question this page doesn't settle.
