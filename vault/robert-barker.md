Robert Barker (died 1645) was the King's Printer whose name stands on the title page of the 1611 King James Bible. He also printed the 1602 Bishops' Bible that the translators worked from, and his name is on the 1631 octavo that told readers "Thou shalt commit adultery." He died in the King's Bench prison. The Muse stops here because the physical history of the KJV, meaning its first issues, its best-known errors and variants, and the fines that followed, runs through his press.

## Historical Context

The 1611 title page ends: "Imprinted at London by Robert Barker, Printer to the Kings most Excellent Maiestie. Anno Dom. 1611." (Pollard's transcription; Scrivener 1884 and the Wikisource transcription of the 1611 scan agree.) The New Testament has its own title page with Barker's imprint.

The book appeared with very little paperwork. Pollard (1911) writes: "The Bible of 1611, being only a revised edition, was not entered on the Stationers' Registers, nor have we any information as to the month in which it was issued." Cambridge University Library says only that it "was printed by the King's Printer at some point in 1611." No month or day is documented.

No authorising document has been found behind the title page's claim either. Pollard: "no authority has ever been discovered for the words 'Appointed to be read in Churches' which appear on the title-page of all editions, nor for the phrase, the 'Authorized Version'."

## Life & Work

**Family.** His birth date hasn't been found. The DNB gives none, and Henry Plomer's 1907 *Dictionary of the Booksellers and Printers* describes him as "The eldest son of Christopher Barker the first, printer to Queen Elizabeth." He was made free of the Stationers' Company by right of his father on 25 June 1589. The DNB: "was made free of the Stationers' Company, per patrimonium, 25 June 1589" (Plomer gives the same date).

**The patent.** The office came to him from his father. The DNB: "The court of assistants of the Stationers' Company recognised, 3 Jan. 1599–1600, the letters patent of Queen Elizabeth of 8 Aug. 1589, granting him the reversion for life, after his father's death, of the office of queen's printer, with right of printing English bibles, books of common prayer, statutes, and proclamations." Until 1752 the English year began on 25 March, so a January date is often written with both years: "1599–1600" is January 1600 by the modern count. Plomer adds: "On the accession of James I, Robert Barker held the office of King's Printer by virtue of the reversionary patent granted to his father". On 10 May 1603, the DNB says, James granted the reversion of the office in turn to Barker's eldest son, Christopher (DNB only).

**The Stationers.** Plomer: "In 1605 and 1606 he was Master of the Company of Stationers." (Plomer is the only source for this.)

**The 1602 Bishops' Bible.** The annotated copy of the 1602 Bishops' Bible at the Bodleian (Arch. A b.18) is a folio printed by Barker. The Bodleian's record says forty unbound copies of that edition went to the translators "to serve as the basis for their translation." See [[bishops-bible]].

**The 1611 folio.** The DNB: "The most important publication we owe to him was the first edition of the authorised version of the English bible of 1611." Plomer also names him as its printer. The first edition is a folio (Scrivener 1884; a Bonhams description of one copy gives "Folio (407 x 276 mm)"). It is Herbert 309 and STC 2216 (Newberry; Princeton). The DNB notes: "Two issues, both handsome folios, were produced in the same year." Scrivener found that "at least two separate issues bear the date of that year, yet differ from each other in so many minute particulars."

**The "He" and "She" Bibles.** The best-known difference is at Ruth 3:15. The Newberry Library describes its copy as "The first issue of the first edition of the Authorized version or King James' Bible, sometimes called the great "He Bible" (Ruth iii, 15: "he went into the citie." In the second issue of 1611, called the "She Bible" and in following editions, this passage reads "she went."" The Queens' College Old Library and the Garrett-Evangelical library guide say the same. There is a twist: the received Hebrew text has the masculine form. Lindsey Askin of Queens' writes that it "reads ויבא העיר 'And he went to the city.'" So "he" matches the Hebrew and "she" is the change. Whether to call the "he" reading an error at all is disputed; it is safer to call it a variant.

**The Wicked Bible.** In 1631 an octavo appeared with the imprint "London: Robert Barker and the assigns of John Bill, 1631. 8vo." (Princeton handlist, STC 2296). Paul Needham's label reads: "its compositor set the seventh Commandment as "Thou shalt commit adultery."" The Museum of the Bible's record says "Barker was fined for the error and most copies of this edition were either corrected or destroyed." Scrivener names the King's Printers held responsible as "Robert Barker and Martin Lucas," and so does the Folger curator Steven Galbraith: "the book's printers, Robert Barker and Martin Lucas, were severely fined." Note the difference: the imprint names Bill's assigns, while the proceedings name Lucas. The full story, with its disputed court and census of copies, is on [[wicked-bible]].

**Losing the business.** Barker spent years fighting Bonham Norton and John Bill for control of the King's Printing House, and the sources date that fight differently (see below). Plomer says "In 1634 Robert Barker mortgaged his moiety of the office to Miles Fletcher and his partners" (Plomer only).

**Prison and death.** The DNB: "on 27 Nov. 1635 he was committed into the custody of the marshal of the king's bench." Plomer says it was for debt: "in 1635 he was commined as a debtor to the King's Bench Prison, where he died in 1645" (OCR; "commined" is "committed"). He never came out. The DNB: "Barker remained in the King's Bench prison until his death, which took place on 10 Jan. 1644–5." That is an Old Style date, so 10 January 1645 by the modern year. Plomer confirms the year; the day comes from the DNB only.

## Claims & Disputes

- **Contested: who paid for the 1611 printing.** Plomer grants that Barker is usually supposed to have paid for it himself, then says "there is little doubt that he was financed throughout by Bonham Norton, John Norton, and John Bill, in return for a share in the profits of the office". That is Plomer's revision of the older view, and he is the only source for it here.
- **Contested: what Barker spent on the 1611 Bible.** Cambridge University Library: "Robert Barker, is estimated to have spent £3,000 on preparation and printing." Pollard cites a 1651 statement (William Ball) that Barker "paid for the amended or corrected Translation of the Bible ;i^3,5oo" (OCR for £3,500), then adds: "we have really no sound basis even for guessing how the ;£3,500 was paid." Neither figure is secure.
- **Contested: when Norton and Bill took over.** The DNB says "The rights were assigned by the Barkers to Bonham Norton and John Bill in 1627, and the assignment was confirmed by the king." Plomer puts an assignment soon after 1615, followed by years of litigation: "In i6t8 Robert Barker brought an action in the Court of Chancery" (OCR "i6t8" is 1618). The two chronologies haven't been reconciled.
- **Contested: which 1611 issue came first.** The Newberry, Queens', and Garrett sources all call the "he" issue the first. Scrivener discussed the priority of the two issues at length, and his own verdict has not yet been read off the page scans for this site.
- **Contested: was "Appointed to be read in Churches" authorised?** Lord Selborne argued that the King's printer would not have printed it falsely: "Nothing in my opinion, is less likely than that the King's printer should have taken upon himself … to issue the book … with a title-page asserting that it was 'Appointed to be read in Churches ' if the fact were not really so." Selborne also suggested an Order in Council, noting that the Council registers for 1600-1613 were lost: "all the Council books and registers from the year 1600 to 1613 inclusive were destroyed by a fire." Pollard's reply is that the Bishops' Bibles of 1585-1602 had carried stronger wording with no known order behind them: "it seems therefore far from safe to postulate the existence of such an Order in 1611." Westcott (1905): "no evidence has yet been produced to shew that the version was ever publicly sanctioned by Convocation or by Parliament, or by the Privy Council, or by the king."
- **Contested: the Wicked Bible fine.** Scrivener says "the real fine was ;^300, to be expended on a fount of fair Greek type" (OCR ";^" for £), imposed in the High Commission. The DNB puts it in the Star Chamber: "The Star Chamber fined Barker 200l., and Lucas 100l." Princeton's label gives the popular version, and hedges it: "It is said that when the mistake was noticed the printers were called before the Star Chamber and fined the large sum of ₤300." The historian Chris Jones splits the fine and says it went unpaid: "He's fined about £200." … "His colleague who he's printing with, Martin Lucas, is fined another £100." … "So, £300 in total. He never pays it." (Jones is a single source, from a podcast interview.)
- **Speculation: the Wicked Bible was sabotage.** Needham reports: "One book trade historian has suggested that a kind of industrial spy intentionally committed the error, to bring into discredit (but unsuccessfully) the Barker family's Bible patent." Fine Books & Collections, reporting a Bonhams sale in 2015, goes further: "research has revealed that the mistake may have been a deliberate act of sabotage by a rival printer." Neither source names the historian or sets out the evidence, so this stays a documented suggestion.
