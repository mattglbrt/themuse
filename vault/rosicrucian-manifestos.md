Three books put the brotherhood of the Rosy Cross into print: the *Fama Fraternitatis*, printed in 1614, the *Confessio*, added to it in 1615, and the *Chymische Hochzeit* of 1616. They arrived three years after the King James Bible, and within two years a London physician, [[robert-fludd]], was defending the brotherhood in print. Fludd also addressed a manuscript defence of it to [[james-i|James I]]. The Muse stops here because the manifestos are where the esoteric story reaches James's court after the Bible was done, and because [[frances-yates]] later wrote a book called *The Rosicrucian Enlightenment*.

## Historical Context

The manifestos came out while learned magic still held the serious attention of Europe's thinkers. Brian Copenhaver describes magic after its late fifteenth-century revival as kept "at the center of cultural attention for nearly two hundred years, as great philosophers and prominent naturalists tried to understand or confirm or reject it" (Copenhaver, "Magic," 2006, chapter abstract).

The page leans on an old source for its dates: Hermann Gruber's article "Rosicrucians" in the *Catholic Encyclopedia* (1912). Gruber wrote from a polemical Catholic position on secret societies, so he's used here only for when things were printed. One of those dates is confirmed by a surviving copy.

## The Document

**Printing history.** Gruber says the *Fama* "first appeared in print in 1614 at Cassel." He goes on: "Beginning with the fourth edition in 1615, the third Rosicrucian rudiment, 'Confessio der Fraternitat', was added to the 'Fama'." And the *Chymische Hochzeit* is the one "which appeared in 1616."

The 1614 Kassel date rests on Gruber alone. The 1615 pairing of the two texts is confirmed by a real book: a Danzig printing of that year, now in the Getty Research Institute and scanned on archive.org. Its title reads "Fama fraternitatis, oder, Entdeckung der Brüderschafft des löblichen Ordens dess Rosen Creutzes : beneben der Confession, oder, Bekantnus derselben Fraternitet", and its imprint "Gedruckt zu Dantzigk : Durch Andream Hünefeldt".

**Defenders.** Gruber: "In the seventeenth century Michael Maier and Robert Fludd were its champions." The *Dictionary of National Biography* says Maier brought the movement to Fludd's attention on a visit to London, and it lists Fludd's two Leiden defences, the *Apologia Compendiaria* of 1616 and the *Tractatus Apologeticus* of 1617. Fludd also addressed a manuscript *Declaratio breuis* on the brotherhood's behalf to James I (DNB; date not given).

**Not yet covered.** Who wrote the manifestos, and what they actually say, haven't been researched for this trail. The author field says Anonymous for that reason, not as a finding, and the page makes no claim about their contents. The Danzig facsimile is the place to start.

## Claims & Disputes

**Established as Yates's framing, contested as history: a "Rosicrucian Enlightenment".** Frances Yates's *The Rosicrucian Enlightenment* (1972) has a chapter titled "THE ROSICRUCIAN MANIFESTOS", in a book that runs from "A ROYAL WEDDING" through "JOHN DEE AND THE RISE OF 'CHRISTIAN ROSENCREUTZ'" to "FROM THE INVISIBLE COLLEGE TO THE ROYAL SOCIETY" (chapter records, Routledge eBook). Her publisher presents the book as "A history of the role that the occult has played in the formation of modern science and medicine" (Routledge publisher's page). That that's what the book argues is established. Whether it's true is contested: the wider claim that occultism drove the rise of modern science is one of the most disputed parts of Yates's work (see [[yates-thesis]]). The line from [[john-dee]] to the manifestos is Yates's argument, and it's attributed to her here.

## Where the Muse Leads

- [[robert-fludd]]: the London physician who defended the brotherhood in 1616 and 1617, and in a manuscript addressed to James.
- [[james-i]]: Fludd's manuscript defence of the Rosicrucians was addressed to him.
- [[frances-yates]]: she gave the manifestos a chapter in *The Rosicrucian Enlightenment*, between Dee and the Royal Society.
- [[yates-thesis]]: her claim that this material fed modern science is the part later scholars fight over.
- [[john-dee]]: Yates's chapter title puts him at the rise of "Christian Rosencreutz". Her argument, worth testing.
