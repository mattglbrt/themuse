# Casaubon on the Hermetica, 1614: what he argued, where, and how scholars read it

**Date:** 2026-09-22 · **Slug:** casaubon-hermetica-1614 · **Log:** `research/logs/2026-09-22-casaubon-hermetica-1614.log.md` · **Sources:** `research/sources/2026-09-22-casaubon-hermetica-1614.json`

## The question

Isaac Casaubon, *De rebus sacris et ecclesiasticis exercitationes XVI* (London, 1614). What did he argue about the date and authorship of the Corpus Hermeticum?
(a) Which section treats Hermes, and on which printed pages?
(b) What is his conclusion in his own Latin, and whose translation is it?
(c) What grounds did he give?
(d) How do modern scholars characterise and qualify the argument?

This memo supersedes rows 27–28 of `research/2026-09-22-hermeticism-cabala-england.md`. Row 27 is now resolved from the page images. Row 28 (Grafton) is still needs-print-check.

## Short answer

- **(a)** Exercitatio I, section **X**, printed **pp. 70–87** of the London 1614 edition (Ex officina Nortoniana apud Ioan. Billium). It answers Baronius's *Apparatus* Num. XVIII. Section X covers the Sibylline oracles, Hydaspes and Hermes together. Section XI starts on p. 87. Page numbers were read from the running heads on the Internet Archive page images, and the item's page-number map agrees. Scan index n122 is p. 70, n123 is p. 71, n138 is p. 86, n139 is p. 87.
- **(b)** Two passages carry the verdict. p. 75: the book "is pseudepigraphal: the mere fiction of some Christian, or better, of a half-Christian" (*Christiani alicuius, vel vt dicam melius, semichristiani merum figmentum*). p. 87, the closing sentence of the section: it is "most false" that these writings are by Mercurius Trismegistus, "the most ancient sage of Egypt", or translated from his works, and "we have proved each of these to be by far the most absurd." The exact Latin is in rows 3–4. **No published English translation was found.** The translations in this memo are working translations by the research assistant and need checking by a Latinist before publication.
- **(c)** He gives four kinds of argument:
  - **Content.** The doctrine is "partly Greek, from the books of Plato and the Platonists, often in their very words", and "partly Christian, taken from the sacred books". Poimandres, "Pastor", is Christ, the Good Shepherd of John.
  - **Anachronism.** The book mentions the sculptor Phidias and the citharode Eunomus of Locri, who won at the Pythian games. Both belong to periods long after the supposed Hermes.
  - **Language.** The style is foreign to the Greek of Hermes's supposed contemporaries. The vocabulary includes words that not even the Greek of the time of Christ's birth recognises. The texts were composed in Greek and are not translations from Egyptian.
  - **General.** Christian mysteries were hidden before Christ, yet these books state them plainly. The old Greeks knew none of this. The early Church had a custom of forging books "for a good end". Such writings were suspected as soon as they appeared.

  He dates the forger to "those first centuries of nascent Christianity" and gives no year. He places the real Egyptian Mercurius around the year 2000 from the creation of the world.
- **(d)** Fetched modern scholarship states the following. Casaubon "questioned the antiquity of the Hermetic writings" and thought "the Hermetic texts were written by Christians" (Van den Kerchove 2014). He "proved in 1614 that they were late texts", **following several sixteenth-century philologists** (Audureau 2019). The 1614 polemic dealt Hermetism "a very serious blow" (Scarpi 2012). Mulsow's 2002 volume documents the dating debate "from Genebrard to Casaubon (1567–1614)", according to the title as cited by Audureau. Modern dating puts the Corpus Hermeticum in the 2nd–3rd centuries AD, with Poimandres possibly late 1st to mid-2nd (Van den Kerchove 2014; Scott 1924 gives mostly 3rd century). So his *late, post-Christian dating* stands in outline. Scarpi notes that precise dating is still "problematic". His *Christian-forger* thesis is not how the fetched modern source describes the texts: Van den Kerchove has them "mixing Greek and Egyptian traditions". I found **no fetched source that explicitly evaluates** his Christian-authorship claim. That is Grafton's and Copenhaver's territory, and both are needs-print-check.

## The Latin and working translations

**These translations are the research assistant's own, unpublished.** They are for orientation only. No published English translation of these passages was fetched. Grafton 1983 probably translates some of them, but that is unverified. Have a Latinist check them before quoting on the channel, or quote Grafton once his article has been checked in print.

- **p. 75 (row 3):** "...*non veremur pronuntiare, & omni asseueratione confirmare, esse ψευδεπίγραφον: vtpote qui sit Christiani alicuius, vel vt dicam melius, semichristiani merum figmentum.*"
  Working translation: "...we are not afraid to declare, and to affirm with every assurance, that it is pseudepigraphal, being the mere fiction of some Christian, or, to put it better, of a half-Christian."
- **p. 87 (row 4):** "*Falsum igitur est, imò falsissimum, quod iste planus in epistola ad Ammonem persuadere vult nobis; à Mercurio prius Aegyptiaco sermone ista fuisse conscripta. Falsissimum etiam est quod docti indocti videntur hactenus credidisse; aut scripta hæc fuisse à Mercurio Trismegisto, vetustissimo Ægypti sapiente; aut ex illius scriptis esse versa. quorum vtrumque probauimus esse longe absurdissimum.*"
  Working translation: "It is false, then, indeed most false, what this impostor tries to persuade us of in the letter to Ammon: that these things were first written by Mercurius in the Egyptian tongue. Most false too is what learned and unlearned alike seem to have believed until now: either that these writings were by Mercurius Trismegistus, the most ancient sage of Egypt, or that they were translated from his writings. We have proved both of these to be by far the most absurd."
- **p. 77 (row 5):** "*primis illis seculis nascentis Christianismi, quum tanta licentia libri falsis titulis inscripti quotidie fingerentur; extitisse aliquem sacris nostræ pietatis leuiter imbutum...*"
  Working translation: "in those first centuries of nascent Christianity, when books under false titles were being forged daily with such licence, [why wonder] that there arose someone lightly imbued with the sacred things of our faith..."

## Source-quality notes

- **Primary.** The 1614 London edition, read from the page images on Internet Archive (item `bub_gb_D23scf3zIVcC`, Google scan of the Biblioteca Nazionale Centrale di Roma copy, Public Domain Mark). This settles (a), (b) and (c) directly. The page images for pp. 70, 71, 74, 75, 77, 86 and 87 were read. Pp. 72–73, 76 and 78–85 were **not** read: they hold the close reading of the Poimander and the hymns, which OCR hits at scans 131–133 show in part. The page numbers apply to this London 1614 edition only. Later editions (e.g. Frankfurt) will paginate differently. That has not been checked.
- **Scholarly secondary, peer-reviewed.** Van den Kerchove (RHR 2014), Audureau (RHR 2019) and Scarpi (Annuaire EPHE 2012) are all French academic venues on OpenEdition, "Tous droits réservés". They are cited here with short quotes only. They are independent of each other. None is a dedicated study of Casaubon: each mentions him in passing.
- **Older scholarly.** Walter Scott's *Hermetica* I (Clarendon, 1924). It was the standard edition in its day but is now superseded (Nock–Festugière, Copenhaver). It is used here for the long view and as an older dating benchmark only.
- **Caution on VdK n. 12.** Her footnote gives Casaubon's etymology as "Isaac Casaubon, Cohortationes in Gentiles 57, 1614", citing R. Marcus, JNES 8 (1949). That reference does not match the *Exercitationes*, where the Poimandres = Pastor = Christ identification sits at p. 77. It looks like a garbled or second-hand citation. Cite the *Exercitationes* p. 77 directly, not her footnote.
- **Not reached (key gaps for (d)).** Grafton 1983 (JSTOR failed to load). Copenhaver 1992 Introduction (only the first-page extract is visible). Mulsow ed. 2002 (no accessible text). Yates 1964 ch. 21 (Internet Archive lending copy is access-restricted and search-inside returned 403). Hanegraaff's "How Hermetic was Renaissance Hermetism?" (Aries 2015, CC-BY on Zenodo): the PDF was fetched but no text could be extracted in this environment. It is worth a manual read, because it is an open-access modern account.
