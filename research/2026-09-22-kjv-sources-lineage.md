# What the KJV descends from: Tyndale, the Textus Receptus, the Vulgate and the Septuagint

## The question

(a) Tyndale's share of the KJV wording: which percentage figures circulate (~83%, ~84%, ~76%, "90%", "nine-tenths"), who measured them, how, and where they were published.
(b) The Greek text lineage behind the KJV New Testament: Erasmus, Stephanus, Beza, and the "Textus Receptus" label.
(c) What the Latin Vulgate and the Septuagint actually did in the translation, and what else the translators say they consulted.

## Headline findings

1. **The "83%" and "84%" figures are one study at two stages.** On 4 June 1997 David Daniell announced at the Library of Congress that "83 percent of the King James New Testament is Tyndale exactly," and credited a BYU student who compared the texts by computer (row 3). That student work is Jon (also printed "John") Nielson's 1994 BYU MA thesis (row 4). The peer-reviewed version, Nielson & Skousen, *Reformation* 3 (1998): 49-74, reports **about 84% for the NT and about 76% for the OT portions Tyndale translated**, based on 18 sampled passages (rows 1-2). "83%" is the pre-publication figure. I found no fetched source for 83% as the published number. The thesis-to-article path is my inference from the dates; mark it "(inferred)" if used.
2. **The "90%" / "nine-tenths" figures are older, and none of them come from a measurement I could find.** Nielson & Skousen give "up to 90 per cent (Westcott)" as the top of earlier estimates (row 1). I could not find the Westcott wording myself (row 10). "Nine-tenths" appears, with no source given, in the New Schaff-Herzog encyclopedia (J. I. Mombert, vol. XII) (row 8). In 1995 Ronald Mansbridge reported that Daniell had said "nine-tenths" of the KJV NT is Tyndale's (row 7).
3. **Other measurements disagree with 84%.** Mansbridge (1995) counted words in nine chapters and got "more than 81%" for the KJV (row 7). Butterworth (1941) got 18% at the low end, and Nielson & Skousen say his method was flawed (rows 1, 6, 11). A 2019 UTA PhD dissertation (Joul Layne Smith) gets 55%, and its method is not visible in the accessible abstract (row 9). **Tier: Contested.** 84/76 is the best-documented figure. It is not the only one.
4. **Greek lineage (Established):** Erasmus 1516 (Basel, Froben, a Latin-Greek edition built on a handful of late manuscripts) → Stephanus 1550 (first critical apparatus) → Beza (editions 1565-1604). Scrivener (1881) judged Beza's 1598 text the one "most likely" in the translators' hands and listed about 190 places where the KJV departs from it. "Textus Receptus" comes from the preface of the 1633 Elzevir edition and was applied to the earlier editions after the fact (rows 12-21).
5. **Vulgate and Septuagint:** the translators' own preface says they translated from the Hebrew (OT) and Greek (NT). They say they consulted "Chaldee, Hebrewe, Syrian, Greeke, or Latine" translators and commentators, plus Spanish, French, Italian and Dutch ones. They also say the Septuagint "dissenteth from the Originall in many places" (rows 22-25). The Vulgate got in by other routes: Erasmus's back-translation of Revelation 22:16-21 (rows 14-15), places where the KJV "exactly follows the Latin Vulgate" (Scrivener, row 20), and Vulgate-derived words that came through the Rheims NT (Carleton, row 28). The official base text was the Bishops' Bible, with Tyndale, Matthew, Coverdale, the Great Bible (Whitchurch) and Geneva to be used where they "agree better with the text" (row 26).
6. **Not found:** which printed Hebrew edition the OT translators used (e.g. Bomberg/ben Hayyim), and a scholarly source for the Apocrypha being translated from the Septuagint, with 2 Esdras from Latin. Both are open items (rows 31-32).
