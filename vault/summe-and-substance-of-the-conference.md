*The Summe and Substance of the Conference* is William Barlow's printed account of the [[hampton-court-conference|Hampton Court Conference]], published in London in 1604. The Muse stops here because it is the primary record of the day the [[king-james-bible]] was proposed. The request for a new translation, Bancroft's objection, James's call for "one vniforme translation," and his attack on the Geneva notes all reach us through this book.

## Historical Context

The conference answered the puritan [[millenary-petition]], which had been presented to [[james-i|James]] on his way to London in April 1603 (Gee & Hardy 1896). It met in January 1604, on the 14th, 16th, and 18th according to the CCEL encyclopedia article on the conference. Barlow's text confirms the first two days.

## The Document

The imprint reads "London: Printed by Iohn Windet, for Mathew Law, 1604" (EEBO-TCP A04434). The title dates the conference "Ianuary 14. 1603." That is Old Style, when the year began on 25 March, so it is January 1604 in modern reckoning. The Internet Archive catalogues its facsimile as "at Hampton Court, January 14, 1603 (1604)."

Three copies of the text are easy to reach:

- The Text Creation Partnership transcription of the 1604 print (EEBO-TCP A04434), free under CC0. This page quotes from it.
- An 1804 reprint (Clerkenwell: Bye and Law), Library of Congress copy, free on the Internet Archive. Its wording matches the 1604 text in modernized spelling. It is a second copy of the same book, so it confirms the text, not the events.
- A 1965 facsimile of the 1604 edition (Scholars' Facsimiles & Reprints), borrow-only on the Internet Archive.

Barlow doesn't write as a neutral recorder, and his asides show it. He says Bancroft "well added" his objection and calls Rainolds's examples "triuiall and old." The speeches in the book are what Barlow printed, not a transcript.

## Contents

The quotations below are from the TCP transcription, in its spelling. Printed page numbers haven't been checked against the facsimile yet, so none are given.

**The second day.** "ON Munday, Ianuary, 16. betweene 11. and 12. of the Clocke, were the foure Plaintiffes called into the Priuie Chamber." The four puritans were Reynolds (Rainolds), Sparkes, Knewstubs, and Chaderton, with "D. Reynalds the Foreman."

**The proposal.** [[john-rainolds|Rainolds]] asked for a new Bible:

> "After that, he moued his Maiestie, that there might bee a newe translation of the Bible, because, those which were allowed in the raignes of Henrie the eight, and Edward the sixt, were corrupt and not aunswerable to the truth of the Originall."

His examples were Gal. 4:25, Ps. 105:28, and Ps. 106:30: "Secondly, Psalme, 105. 28. they were not obedient; The Originall beeing, They were not disobedient." Barlow calls them "triuiall and old, and alreadie, in print, often aunswerred."

**The objection.** From [[richard-bancroft|Bancroft]], Bishop of London: "onely, my Lord of London well added, that if euery mans humour should be followed, there would be no ende of translating."

**The king's answer.**

> "VVhereupon his Highnesse wished, that some especiall paines should be taken in that behalfe for one vniforme translation (professing that hee could neuer, yet, see a Bible well translated in English; but the worst of all, his Maiestie thought the Geneua to bee)"

The work was to be "done by the best learned in both the Vniuersities, after them to bee reuiewed by the Bishops, and the chiefe learned of the Church; from them to bee presented to the Priuie-Councell; and lastly to bee ratified by his Royall authoritie; and so this whole Church to be bound vnto it, and none other."

**The ban on notes.**

> "Marry, withall, hee gaue this caueat (vpon a word cast out by my Lord of London) that no marginall notes should be added"

> "hauing found in them, which are annexed to the Geneua translation (which he sawe in a Bible giuen him by an English Lady) some notes very partiall, vntrue, seditious, and sauouring too much, of daungerous, and trayterous conceites"

> "As for example, Exod. 1. 19. where the marginall note alloweth disobedience to Kings. And 2. Chron. 15. 16. the note taxeth Asa for deposing his mother, onely, and not killing her"

The book doesn't say which edition of the [[geneva-bible|Geneva Bible]] the English lady gave James.

## Claims & Disputes

- **Established:** that James, as Barlow records him, called the Geneva notes "seditious" and named Exod. 1:19 and 2 Chron. 15:16.
- **Contested:** whether the notes were seditious. Tom Furniss (2009) notes that the Geneva Bible "has been considered by many as a revolutionary or seditious text, especially because of the numerous explanatory notes," and argues that "its politics are undecidable" (abstract). See [[hampton-court-conference]] for both positions in full.
- **Barlow's reliability:** Barlow is our main witness and he takes sides in his own text. Frederick Shriver's 1982 article, "Hampton Court Re-visited," revisited the conference; its argument hasn't been read for this page.
