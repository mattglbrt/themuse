The North Berwick witch trials were a Scottish witch hunt of 1590-91 in which people were tortured into confessing to a plot against the king, then tried and killed. The Muse stops here because the king was in the room. [[james-i|James VI]], who later put his name on the [[king-james-bible]], questioned the accused himself, and a few years later wrote a book arguing that witches should be punished.

## Historical Context

The University of Edinburgh's *Survey of Scottish Witchcraft* describes the case of Agnes Sampson as "Part of the North Berwick hunt, that revovled around witchcraft and treason" (spelling as in the Survey; case C/EGD/63). Treason is the key word: the accused were said to have worked against the king. The standard scholarly edition of the documents calls it "the North Berwick witch hunt — in which King James was involved as alleged victim, interrogator, judge, and demonologist" (Normand & Roberts 2000, abstract).

Most of the story as it is usually told comes from one London pamphlet, [[newes-from-scotland|Newes from Scotland]]. Normand and Roberts call it "the most propagandastic of all the texts generated by the North Berwick witch trials" (their spelling). The court records summarised in the Survey give the trials, verdicts, and executions.

## The Idea

What is documented, kept apart from what was confessed.

**How it began, per *Newes*.** David Seaton, deputy bailiff of Tranent, had a servant, [[geillis-duncan|Geillis Duncan]]. He had her tortured "with the torture of the Pilliwinckes vpon her fingers." She then named others, beginning with "Agnis Sampson the eldest Witch of them al, dwelling in Haddington."

**The king as interrogator.** *Newes* says [[agnes-sampson|Agnes Sampson]] "was taken and brought to Haliriud house before the Kings Maiestie and sundry other of the nobility of Scotland, where she was straitly examined." The Survey lists "James (VI) Stewart" among the investigators in her case and records that "She confessed to these things in front of King James VI." *Newes* adds that the king called the accused "extreame lyars," and that Sampson then repeated to him the words he and his queen spoke "at Upslo in Norway the first night of their mariage."

**What was confessed.** *Newes* reports confessions of the Devil appearing at "North Barrick Kerke" in "the habit or likenes of a man," and of a christened cat that caused the king's ship, coming from Denmark, to meet "a contrary winde to the rest of his Ships." These are the contents of confessions made under torture. They are not events.

**The torture and the killing.** [[john-fian|John Fian]], also called John Cunningham, a schoolmaster at Saltpans, was tortured with "the bootes" (*Newes*). The Survey records his trial on 26 December 1590 in the central court at Edinburgh, and a guilty verdict. It records Agnes Sampson's trial on 21 January 1591, a guilty verdict, and her execution on 28 January 1591 on Castle Hill, by the method it records as "Strangle & Burn." *Newes* says Fian was burned on the Castle Hill at the end of January 1591.

## Claims & Disputes

- **Established:** named people confessed under documented torture, some of them in front of the king, and were tried, convicted, and executed (Survey; *Newes*; Normand & Roberts).
- **Established, as confession content only:** the Devil at North Berwick kirk, the christened cat, and the attempt to drown the king at sea. The sources record that these things were confessed. They never show that they happened.
- **Contested: John Fian's death date.** The Survey has his trial (26 December 1590) but no execution date. The only date on this page is the pamphlet's "in the ende of Ianuarie last past. 1591." Other dates circulate online and conflict with each other; none is used here.
