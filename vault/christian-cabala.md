Christian Cabala is the name for Christian scholars taking up Kabbalah, the Jewish mysticism that Pico hoped "could help save" Christian souls. It starts on this trail with [[pico-della-mirandola]] in the 1480s, passes through [[johann-reuchlin]], and reaches England, as far as the sources checked here show, only through [[john-dee]]. The Muse stops here because it's tempting to picture the KJV's England soaked in this learning, and the record is thinner, and more interesting, than that.

## Historical Context

**Pico.** Brian Copenhaver's *Stanford Encyclopedia* entry calls Pico "the first Christian who described Kabbalah in detail or treated it as worthwhile, hoping to persuade other Christians that this Jewish mysticism could help save their souls." Pico's Kabbalah came to him in Latin, and not cleanly: "Flavius, his prolific helper and deceiver, translated and mistranslated thousands of pages of Kabbalah from Aramaic and Hebrew into Latin." Pico "had these Conclusions printed in Rome at the end of 1486", and "Innocent VIII indicted 13 of the 900 theses and appointed a commission that soon declared 6 of them suspect and condemned 7 others."

**Reuchlin.** Johann Reuchlin met Pico "in Italy in 1490". In 1494 "he published in Basel his 'De verbo mirifico' ('The wonder-working word'), his first publication on (Christian) Cabbala" (Gerber, Ritman Research Institute, 2025). His *De arte cabalistica* "was published in 1517 in Hagenau by Anshelm and is dedicated to Pope Leo X" (frommann-holzboog critical edition, publisher's page).

## The Idea

On what's been verified, the idea is Pico's: that Kabbalah was worth a Christian's attention and could help save Christian souls (Copenhaver's summary, above). Reuchlin carried it into books of his own in 1494 and 1517. The deeper content, what these writers actually took from Kabbalah and did with it, isn't covered here yet.

**In England: Dee.** The one documented English case in the sources checked is John Dee. His *Monas Hieroglyphica* (Antwerp, 1564) is titled, as the *Dictionary of National Biography* gives it, "'Monas Hieroglyphica, Mathematicè, Magicè, Cabalisticè, Anagogicèque explicata…'": explained, among other ways, cabalistically. Deborah Harkness's Cambridge monograph on Dee's angel conversations is subtitled *Cabala, Alchemy, and the End of Nature* (1999), with a chapter titled "The True Cabala" (in quotation marks), subtitled "Reading the Book of Nature".

## Claims & Disputes

**Established as Yates's framing: Christian Cabala as a key to Elizabethan England.** The picture of an Elizabethan England shaped by Christian Cabala comes largely from [[frances-yates]]. Her *The Occult Philosophy in the Elizabethan Age* (1979) includes the chapters "JOHN DEE: CHRISTIAN CABALIST" and "SHAKESPEARE AND CHRISTIAN CABALA: FRANCESCO GIORGI AND THE MERCHANT OF VENICE" (chapter records, Routledge eBook). Those readings are hers and belong to her. Her wider framework is contested (see [[yates-thesis]]).

**Contested: Cabala and Hermes as one tradition.** It's easy to run Christian Cabala and Hermeticism together. Copenhaver says Pico, the first Christian to take Kabbalah seriously in detail, didn't: "Imitating Proclus, Pico preferred Chaldean prophets and Orphic bards to the Egyptian Hermes. As for a tradition called 'Hermetic', the Conclusions had nothing good to say" (*SEP*, §2.1). See [[hermeticism]].

**Not found: Christian Cabala among the King James translators.** It would be easy to connect this learning to the men who translated the 1611 Bible. The sources checked don't support it. The *Dictionary of National Biography* articles on three translators (Lancelot Andrewes, John Rainolds, and Edward Lively), Cambridge University Library's 2011 King James Bible exhibition, and the 1611 preface "The Translators to the Reader" contain no mention of cabala, Hermes, magic, occult learning, or Dee. The preface does say the translators consulted "the Translators or Commentators, Chaldee, Hebrewe, Syrian, Greeke, or Latine". That's language scholarship, not Cabala. This is an absence in the sources checked, covering only three of the translators, not proof of a negative.

**Not found: Fludd and Cabala.** No source checked ties [[robert-fludd]] to Kabbalah either. The DNB article on him doesn't use the word.

## Where the Muse Leads

- [[pico-della-mirandola]]: the first Christian to describe Kabbalah in detail and treat it as worthwhile.
- [[johann-reuchlin]]: met Pico in 1490 and put Christian Cabala into print in 1494 and 1517.
- [[john-dee]]: the one documented English case, from the title page of the *Monas* to his angel conversations.
- [[frances-yates]]: the historian who read Elizabethan England, Dee and Shakespeare included, through Christian Cabala.
- [[yates-thesis]]: whether that reading holds up is part of the larger fight over her work.
- [[hermeticism]]: the other thread people tie this to, and Copenhaver's reason for keeping them apart in Pico.
