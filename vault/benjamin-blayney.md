Benjamin Blayney was the Oxford scholar who prepared the corrected edition of the King James Bible that the Clarendon Press published in 1769. Between F. S. Parris's Cambridge edition of 1762 and Blayney's Oxford edition of 1769, the KJV text was brought to an almost complete standard form, and the publisher's description of David Norton's textual history calls the 1769 text "the text we know today." The Muse stops here because the "King James Bible" people quote is not quite the book of 1611. It is the book after a century and a half of printers and editors, and Blayney's edition is where that settled.

## Historical Context

The KJV text moved around in print from the start. The first issue of the 1611 folio reads "he went into the citie" at Ruth 3:15, and the second issue of 1611 and later editions read "she went" (Newberry Library holdings list). The received Hebrew text has the masculine form, so the "he" of the first issue follows the Hebrew and the "she" is the change (Askin 2012; Sefaria). Twenty years on, the 1631 [[wicked-bible|Wicked Bible]] printed the seventh commandment as "Thou shalt commit adultery." (Princeton 2004.)

David Norton's *A Textual History of the King James Bible* (Cambridge University Press, 2005) is described by its publisher as tracing "how, for better and for worse, it changed in the hands of printers and editors until, in 1769, it became the text we know today." Reviewing Norton in *Themelios*, P. J. Williams sums up the result as "an almost complete standardisation of the text between 1762 and 1769 under F.S. Parris (Master of Sidney Sussex, Cambridge) and Benjamin Blayney (Regius Professor of Hebrew, Oxford)".

## Life & Work

**Dates and family.** The *Dictionary of National Biography* (Henry Bradley, 1885-1900) heads its entry "BLAYNEY, BENJAMIN, D.D. (1728–1801), Hebrew scholar". His exact birth date hasn't been found. Joseph Foster's *Alumni Oxonienses* gives his father: "Blayney, Benjamin, s. Thomas, of Worcester (city), gent." (Foster is the only source for that.)

**Oxford.** He went up to Worcester College. Foster: "Worcester Coll., matric. 1 July, 1746, aged 18; B.A. 1750, M.A. 1753". The DNB agrees on the degrees: he "was first a member of Worcester College, Oxford, where he graduated B.A. in 1750 and M.A. in 1753." Then Hertford. The DNB: "He afterwards became fellow, and eventually vice-principal, of Hertford College, and took the degree of B.D. in 1768." Foster lists the same fellowship, the B.D. of 1768, and the office of "vice-principal Hertford College".

**Hebrew.** The DNB lists his Hebrew work, and these titles and dates rest on the DNB alone. "In 1775 he published 'A Dissertation by way of Inquiry into the true Import and Application of the Vision related, Dan. ix. 24 to the End, usually called Daniel's Prophecy of Seventy Weeks.'" Then: "In 1784 Blayney published a new translation of Jeremiah and Lamentations". And: "In 1790 he published an edition of the Hebrew-Samaritan Pentateuch".

**The 1769 edition.** The DNB says: "He was employed by the Clarendon Press to prepare a corrected edition of the authorised version of the Bible. This edition, which has received very high praise for its accuracy, appeared in 1769." Foster says the same without the year: he "edited an edition of the authorized version of the Bible for the Clarendon Press". Scrivener's 1884 list of editions records it as a two-volume quarto and folio printed at Oxford by T. Wright and W. Gill, Printers to the University, in 1769, with the Prayer Book prefixed.

**The Cambridge edition before it.** Seven years earlier Cambridge had produced its own corrected text. Scrivener records it as a two-volume folio and quarto, "Printed by Joseph Bentham, Printer to the Univer^ sity. Sold by Benjamin Dod, Bookseller... London, 1762." (Scrivener 1884, list of editions, no. 14; the "^" is an OCR artifact in the archive.org text.) Its editor appears as "Parris" in Norton and Williams and as "Paris" in Scrivener. His full given names haven't been confirmed from a source yet, so this page uses F. S. Parris.

**Blayney's own account.** Blayney explained his method in a report to the Clarendon Press. Scrivener calls it "a very interesting account of his work and the principles upon which it was executed in a brief Report to the Vice-Chancellor and Delegates of the Clarendon Press, reprinted below (p. 238) as Appendix D" (Scrivener 1884, pp. 28–29). The report is addressed "To the Rev. the Vice- Chancellor, and the other Delegates of the Clarendon Press." and signed from "Hertford College, Oct. 25, 1769." It has been read for this page only in passages, through archive.org's text search of the scan. What those passages show:

- **What he compared.** He writes that "the folio edition of 1611, that of 1701, published under the direction of Bishop Lloyd [see above, p. 26], and two Cambridge editions of a late date, one in quarto, the other in octavo, have been carefully collated". The bracketed cross-reference is Scrivener's.
- **The originals and the italics.** "Frequent recourse has been had to the Hebrew and Greek Originals; and as on other occasions, so with a special regard to the words not expressed in the Original Language, but which our Translators have thought fit to insert in Italics". On the italics he gives credit to an earlier editor: "And though Dr Paris [see above, p. 28] made large corrections in this particular in an edition".
- **Who checked his changes.** He "submitted them all to the previous examination of the Select Committee, and particularly of the Principal of Hertford College1, and Mr Professor Wheeler." (The "1" is a footnote marker in Scrivener.)
- **The proofs.** "the first proofs carefully collated with the Copy both text and margin; after which the second proofs were again read, and generally speaking, the third likewise".

**The italics.** Not every judgement on his work is praise. Discussing a list of "blunders in regard to the italic type", Scrivener complains that "Blayney is followed in the rest by the whole flock of moderns, without inquiry and without suspicion." (Scrivener 1884, p. 35.) In context that sentence is about the italic type only. It doesn't say modern Bibles follow Blayney in everything.

**His chair.** Williams calls him "Regius Professor of Hebrew." The DNB: "He was appointed regius professor of Hebrew in 1787, and in the same year was made canon of Christ Church and received the degree of D.D." Foster gives the same year: "canon of Christ Church and D.D. 1787". That is eighteen years after the Bible came out, so he didn't hold the chair when he edited the 1769 text. The DNB's 1904 errata move the words "in 1787" within that sentence. They don't change the year. When the chair ended is less clear (see Claims & Disputes).

**Death.** The DNB: "He died at his rectory of Poulshot, Wiltshire, on 20 Sept. 1801, aged 73." Foster, who calls him "rector of Poulshot, Wilts," gives the same day: "died 20 Sep., 1801, aged 73."

## Claims & Disputes

- **Contested: whose edition mattered more.** Norton's publisher's description gives 1769 as the moment the text became the one "we know today." Scrivener thought Cambridge 1762 deserved more of the credit. Of Parris's edition he wrote that "his book is almost unknown even to Biblical students, although it has contributed more than that which appeared but seven years later towards bringing the text, the marginal annotations, the italics, and the textual references of modern Bibles into their actual condition." (Scrivener 1884, p. 29.) That is one scholar's judgement.
- **Contested: the fire.** Two sources tell a fire story, and they burn different books. Scrivener says of the Cambridge edition: "Paris's edition had no real circulation, partly because it was so soon superseded by Blayney's, chiefly by reason of a large portion of the impression having been destroyed by fire in Dod's the publisher's warehouse." (Scrivener 1884, p. 29.) The DNB says of Blayney's 1769 edition: "Unfortunately a large part of the impression was destroyed in a fire which took place at the Bible Warehouse in Paternoster Row, and copies are now scarce." The two accounts disagree, and neither has been checked against a modern study. This page doesn't merge them into one story.
- **Contested: when his chair ended.** Two sources print an end date that doesn't fit his death. Foster: "regius prof. Hebrew 1787-1802". Scrivener: "Benjamin Blayney, D.D., Regius Professor of Hebrew, 1787 — 1802." Yet Foster and the DNB both date his death to 20 September 1801. The sources used here don't explain the 1802, so this page gives the start of the chair, 1787, and not the "1787-1802" span.
