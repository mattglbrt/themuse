The Wicked Bible is a 1631 octavo printing of the King James Bible whose seventh commandment reads "Thou shalt commit adultery." The printers were fined, most copies were corrected or destroyed, and uncorrected copies are rare. The Muse stops here because a single dropped word opens up the whole business of printing the KJV: who held the right to print it, who punished their mistakes, and how much of the popular story about this one book is actually on record. Quite a lot of it isn't, and this page tries to say which parts.

## Historical Context

The 1611 title page of the King James Bible reads "Imprinted at London by Robert Barker, Printer to the Kings most Excellent Maiestie. Anno Dom. 1611." (Pollard 1911, transcription of the title page.) Paul Needham's label for Princeton's 2004 exhibition refers to "the Barker family's Bible patent."

Twenty years later [[robert-barker|Barker]] was still one of the King's Printers. F. H. A. Scrivener's 1884 study of the KJV's printings names the pair held responsible for the Wicked Bible as "the King's Printers, Robert Barker and Martin Lucas" (Scrivener 1884, p. 25 n.). Steven Galbraith, Andrew W. Mellon Curator of Books at the Folger, also names "the book's printers, Robert Barker and Martin Lucas" (Galbraith 2011).

## The Document

**Imprint and format.** Princeton's handlist gives the book as "London: Robert Barker and the assigns of John Bill, 1631. 8vo.", STC 2296 (Princeton 2004). The Museum of the Bible's record agrees: "Printed in 1631 by Barker and Assigns of John Bill, London, England." So the title page names Barker and Bill's assigns, while the accounts of the punishment name Barker and Martin Lucas. Both are true on their own terms. The first describes the imprint, the second the men held responsible.

**The print run.** *Fine Books & Collections* (2015) says "The vast majority of the 1,000 copy print run was also destroyed". It gives no source for the 1,000 figure, so treat that number as unconfirmed.

**What happened to the edition.** The Museum of the Bible's catalogue says "Barker was fined for the error and most copies of this edition were either corrected or destroyed." Its own copy is described as "a rare uncorrected copy." According to Needham, "The edition was lost from sight until 1855 when the dealer Henry Stevens found a copy and sold it to James Lenox – the copy now at New York Public Library." (Princeton 2004; one source.)

**Known copies.** The research behind this page found copies documented at Princeton (Scheide Library), the New York Public Library, the Bodleian Library in Oxford (which lent its copy to the Folger in 2011), the Museum of the Bible, the Dunham Bible Museum at Houston Christian University, and the Donnithorne copy at Christchurch, on loan to the University of Canterbury's Macmillan Brown Library. The University of Canterbury Press calls the Donnithorne Bible "the only known copy of this edition (STC 2nd ed 2296) to exist in the southern hemisphere." That list covers only the copies found in the sources and is not a census. How many copies survive in total is disputed and has been revised upward over the years (see below).

## Contents

The text is the King James Bible. What makes this edition famous is one line. In Needham's words: "The "Wicked" Bible is the most famous curiosity among the innumerable editions of the King James Bible: its compositor set the seventh Commandment as "Thou shalt commit adultery."" (Princeton 2004.)

## Claims & Disputes

- **Established: the error and the fine.** The misprinted commandment is documented by Princeton and the Museum of the Bible. The printers were fined. Scrivener gives the total as £300 and says it was "to be expended on a fount of fair Greek type." (Scrivener 1884, p. 25 n.)
- **Contested: which court.** Scrivener places the case in the High Commission. The popular version says the Star Chamber. Even Princeton's label hedges it: "It is said that when the mistake was noticed the printers were called before the Star Chamber and fined the large sum of ₤300, and that the entire edition was recalled and burned." (Princeton 2004.) Houston Christian University's 2024 news story also gives the Star Chamber version. The court is disputed, and this page doesn't pick one.
- **Contested: whether the fine was paid.** Scrivener says the money was meant for Greek type. Chris Jones of the University of Canterbury, who edited the university's digital edition of the Donnithorne copy, says in a podcast interview that Barker was "fined about £200," that "His colleague who he's printing with, Martin Lucas, is fined another £100," and that "So, £300 in total. He never pays it." (Jones, Museum of the Bible podcast, undated.) That is one historian speaking, not yet checked against his published edition.
- **Contested: the date.** The imprint says 1631. Scrivener writes of the book being "published by the King's Printers, Robert Barker and Martin Lucas, in 1632". The date of the proceedings is not settled in the sources used here.
- **Contested: how many copies survive.** The published estimates have gone up over time as more copies have surfaced:
  - 2004, Princeton exhibition catalogue (Needham): "Ten or more copies are now known of the Wicked Bible."
  - 2015, *Fine Books & Collections*, on a Bonhams sale: "only 10 copies believed to have survived to modern times."
  - 2024, Houston Christian University: "It is believed that 11 copies somehow survived"
  - Undated, Chris Jones on the Museum of the Bible podcast: "So far there are 17 copies in institutional hands and in private hands somewhere between four and eight."

  Jones's count is the highest and the most recent-sounding, but it rests on him alone and carries no date. The University of Canterbury's digital edition of the Donnithorne copy, which may hold a census, couldn't be read for this research.
- **Speculation: sabotage.** The claim is that the error was deliberate, planted to embarrass the Barkers. Needham reports it from an unnamed source: "One book trade historian has suggested that a kind of industrial spy intentionally committed the error, to bring into discredit (but unsuccessfully) the Barker family's Bible patent." (Princeton 2004.) *Fine Books & Collections*, reporting a 2015 Bonhams sale, says "research has revealed that the mistake may have been a deliberate act of sabotage by a rival printer". Neither source names the historian or the research. As the record stands, sabotage is a suggestion somebody made, and no evidence for it has been cited.
