# The Apocrypha in the King James Bible (Phase 1, Q5, retry)

Date: 2026-09-22 · Researcher agent · Log: `research/logs/2026-09-22-kjv-apocrypha.log.md` · Sources: `research/sources/2026-09-22-kjv-apocrypha.json`

## The question

1. Where the Apocrypha sat in the 1611 edition, which books, how it was labelled, which translation company did it.
2. When and why it was dropped from printings, who decided, and in what year. Specifically: 17th-century printings without it; Archbishop Abbot's reported 1615 prohibition; the British and Foreign Bible Society (BFBS) decision usually dated 1826.
3. Context: the Church of England's Article VI position.
4. Open item from `2026-09-22-kjv-sources-lineage.md` (row 32): "the Apocrypha was translated from the Greek (Septuagint), and 2 Esdras from Latin."

## Headline findings

- **1611 placement and label (primary facsimile).** The 1611 table of contents lists "Bookes [c]alled Apocrypha" between the Old Testament and "The Bookes of the New Testament": 1 and 2 Esdras, Tobit, Judith, the rest of Esther, Wisdom, Ecclesiasticus, Baruch with the Epistle of Jeremiah, the Song of the Three Children, Susanna, Bel and the Dragon, the Prayer of Manasses, 1 and 2 Maccabees. In the scan, Malachi ends, 1 Esdras begins, pages carry the running head "Apocrypha" through 2 Maccabees, and then comes the New Testament title page with Robert Barker's imprint.
- **Company.** The second Cambridge company did the Apocrypha. Pollard's reprint of the Burnet list, the CUL exhibition, Scrivener and Selden's Table-Talk all say so. John Bois and Andrew Downes were in it.
- **Source texts (open item from the sibling memo, now partly sourced).** Scrivener (Cambridge UP, 1884) says the translators worked from printed Greek Bibles: the Aldine (1518), the Complutensian and the Roman Septuagint of 1586. For 1 Esdras, which the Complutensian lacks, they relied mainly on the Aldine. **2 Esdras** "exists only in the common Latin version", so it was done from the Latin, with Latin manuscripts and the Bishops' Bible carrying weight. The **Prayer of Manasses** also had to come from the Latin, because the Greek was not printed until 1657. So the sibling memo's wording needs a fix. It was "printed Greek editions of the Septuagint, with 2 Esdras *and the Prayer of Manasses* from Latin." This rests on one source (Scrivener).
- **1615 "prohibition": documented, but it was narrower than the usual retelling.** Rivington (1883) wrote a paper on the Stationers' Company records. It says that in 1615 "the Archbishop of Canterbury" "directed public notice to be given that no bibles were to be bound up and sold without the apocrypha on pain of a whole year's imprisonment." That is about *binding and selling*, not printing, and Rivington does not name Abbot. Scrivener (1884) names Abbot and cites Rivington, so Scrivener is not independent. George Abbot was Archbishop from 1611 to 1633 (DNB), so the identification is sound. The underlying Stationers' Company court record was **not seen**, which makes this a needs-print-check.
- **Earliest dropped printings found.** Scrivener describes a London small quarto of 1629 (Bonham Norton and John Bill). It "does not contain the Apocrypha", yet "APO-" still stands below the tail-piece after Malachi. His footnote: "Thus early began the practice of leaving out the Apocrypha, although it had been forbidden by Archbishop Abbot in 1615". This is not a claim that 1629 was the first ever. The first Apocrypha-less KJV printing is **not established** here.
- **"Who decided"?** No single decision to drop the Apocrypha from KJV printings was found. The record shows printers leaving it out from at least 1629, *against* the 1615 order. The famous institutional decision belongs to the BFBS, and it was about the Society's own funds and circulation, not KJV printing generally.
- **BFBS: the decision is November 1825, not 1826.** Canton (1904) is the Society's official history. It dates the resolution to "the 21st November" 1825, at a meeting of the General Committee. Its wording: the fundamental law of the Society, which limits it to the circulation of the Holy Scriptures, is "recognised as excluding the circulation of the Apocrypha". Regulations built on it were "adopted at the annual general meetings in 1826 and 1827." The dispute was over foreign auxiliaries that circulated Bibles with the Apocrypha, with Scottish societies leading the opposition. Canton reports the President's statement that circulating the Apocrypha "in our own versions" had "never been attempted". This rests on one source; Browne's 1859 history could not be read.
- **Article VI.** The Church "doth read" the other books "for example of life and instruction of manners; but yet doth it not apply them to establish any doctrine." The list names 14 books, among them "The Third Book of Esdras" and "The Fourth Book of Esdras" (the KJV's 1 and 2 Esdras; that equivalence is inferred, not sourced here). The Articles were "confirmed again" by Convocation "in the Year of our Lord 1571."

## Source-quality notes

- **Primary:** the 1611 Bible (EEBO microfilm on archive.org, rows 1–2). The OCR of the black-letter microfilm is poor. Quotes are raw OCR, and the page sequence was read from OCR, not from the page images. Before publishing, someone should look at scan images 21, 962 and 1174 by eye. Article VI (rows 20–21) is primary text via two web transcriptions. Wikisource transcribes an 1863 BCP scan; the eskimo.com page gives no edition. The official Church of England page did not deliver the article text to the fetch tool. The BFBS resolution text (row 17) is primary wording, but as reprinted by Canton.
- **Scholarly secondary (19th century, still standard):** Scrivener 1884 (Cambridge UP) is the classic bibliographical study of KJV printings and the only source here for the Apocrypha's source texts (rows 9–10) and the 1629 quarto (row 14). Pollard 1911 (Oxford UP/Frowde) is a documentary collection; its Burnet list and Selden reprint work as primary texts through a secondary editor. DNB 1885 (row 13) is a reference entry and serves only for Abbot's dates.
- **Records-based secondary:** Rivington 1883 (row 11) was a paper by C. R. Rivington on the Stationers' Company records. The fetched copy does not say who Rivington was; the idea that he wrote with access to the Company's own books comes from his title, not from a source. It is the root source for the 1615 order in this memo. Scrivener's Abbot statement comes from it. Treat the 1615 order as **documented once, primary unseen**.
- **Institutional histories:** Canton 1904 is the BFBS's own official history, so it is not neutral on the controversy. The dates and resolution text are probably safe, but the framing ("anti-Apocrypha party", etc.) is the Society's.
- **Institutional/museum:** CUL exhibition captions (row 6) are reliable and independent of Pollard.
- **Maps only (not cited):** Wikipedia "Biblical apocrypha", "Apocrypha", "King James Version", "Deuterocanonical books", "George Abbot (bishop)". The "1826" date on Wikipedia traces in one case to a non-scholarly web page ("Christian heritage fellowship"). Wikipedia's claim that the KJV Apocrypha was "rendered from the Septuagint and Latin Vulgate" traces to a Logos wiki page. Neither was used.

### Corrections this memo supports for page authors

- Say **1825** for the BFBS decision (General Committee, 21 November 1825), with regulations adopted in 1826–27. "In 1826 the Bible Society decided…" compresses this.
- Say the 1615 order was about **binding and selling** Bibles without the Apocrypha, was issued by the Archbishop of Canterbury (Abbot at the time), and is documented via Rivington 1883. Don't say "printing" and don't quote a statute.
- "From the Septuagint" is shorthand. Say "from printed Greek Bibles (the Aldine of 1518, the Complutensian and the Roman edition of 1586), with 2 Esdras and the Prayer of Manasses from the Latin." Attribute it to Scrivener.
- Scrivener's verdict on the company's work ("little favourable can be said") is a quotable scholarly opinion. Attribute it to him; it is not a fact.
