Richard Bancroft (1544-1610) was Bishop of London at the [[hampton-court-conference|Hampton Court Conference]] in 1604, and Archbishop of Canterbury later the same year. The Muse stops here because of a reversal. At Hampton Court he objected to a new translation, saying there would be "no ende of translating." Then his "word cast out" prompted the king's ban on marginal notes, and he went on to organize the companies that made the [[king-james-bible]].

## Historical Context

Bancroft was elected Bishop of London in 1597: "On 21 April 1597 Bancroft was elected" (DNB, "Bancroft, Richard"). When James came to the English throne, puritan ministers met him with the [[millenary-petition]], and he answered by calling the Hampton Court Conference in January 1604. Bancroft sat on the bishops' side of it.

## Life & Work

**Birth and Cambridge.** The DNB says he was "born at Farnworth, Lancashire, in September 1544." Venn's *Alumni Cantabrigienses* gives the same, but Venn cites the DNB for it, so the birth rests on one source. He was a scholar of Christ's College, Cambridge, in 1563. He took the B.A. in 1566-7, an Old Style date that means January to March 1567 by the modern year, then the M.A. from Jesus in 1570 and the D.D. in 1585 (DNB; Venn).

**Bishop of London, 1597-1604.** The *Fasti Ecclesiae Anglicanae* gives the steps: elected 21 April, confirmed by the archbishop 6 May, and consecrated 8 May 1597. The sources differ by a day on his enthronement (see below).

**At Hampton Court.** When [[john-rainolds|Rainolds]] asked for a new translation, William Barlow's account gives Bancroft's answer: "onely, my Lord of London well added, that if euery mans humour should be followed, there would be no ende of translating" (Barlow 1604). The Victorian DNB tells the same moment less kindly, drawing on Barlow: "when Reynolds, on the second day's conference, brought forward a well-sustained proposal for a new translation of the Bible, Bancroft petulantly observed that 'if every man's humour should be followed, there would be no end of translating'" (DNB).

James went ahead anyway, and Bancroft had a hand in the best-known condition he attached:

> "Marry, withall, hee gaue this caueat (vpon a word cast out by my Lord of London) that no marginall notes should be added" (Barlow 1604)

**Archbishop of Canterbury, 1604-1610.** He was elected on 17 November 1604 and confirmed on 10 December (*Fasti*; the DNB has the same election date). In 1608 he was also made Chancellor of the University of Oxford: "In 1608 Bancroft was elected chancellor of the university of Oxford" (DNB).

**Organizing the translation.** Cambridge University Library's 2011 exhibition says "Bancroft sent out lists of membership of the companies in Westminster, Cambridge and Oxford in summer 1604," and that as Archbishop of Canterbury he "presided over the companies of translators of the King James Bible." Pollard (1911) cites "a circular sent out by Bancroft to the other Bishops on July 31" of that year. Once the king had backed the project, the DNB says, the translation "found in him a ready supporter when enforced by the royal sanction; and it is due to Bancroft to recognise the fact that much of the success which ultimately attended that great undertaking was due to his zealous co-operation." How much credit he deserves is the DNB's judgement alone.

**The rules.** The fifteen rules for the translators are often called Bancroft's rules. Rule 1 set the base text: "The ordinary Bible read in the Church, commonly called the Bishops Bible, to be followed, and as little altered as the Truth of the original will permit." Rule 6 repeats the Hampton Court ban, with one exception: "No Marginal Notes at all to be affixed, but only for the Explanation of the Hebrew or Greek Words, which cannot without some circumlocution, so briefly and fitly be express'd in the Text" (Burnet's text, reprinted in Pollard 1911).

**Casaubon.** In 1610 Bancroft, now archbishop, sent the invitation that brought the scholar Isaac Casaubon to England: "On 20 July 1610 an official invitation came to him from the Archbishop of Canterbury (Bancroft)" (DNB, "Casaubon, Isaac").

**Death.** He died on 2 November 1610, before the translation was printed. The DNB: "Bancroft died (after protracted suffering) of the stone 2 Nov. 1610, and was interred in Lambeth Church." The *Fasti* takes the same date from his monument at Lambeth, and Venn agrees. Where he died isn't stated in the sources checked.

## Claims & Disputes

- **Contested (a day's difference): his enthronement at London.** The DNB says "his enthronement took place on 5 June." The *Fasti*, citing a Guildhall manuscript, says "Enthroned 6 June." Both are 1597. The sources haven't been reconciled.
- **Contested (who wrote the rules):** the document itself doesn't name Bancroft. In the text Burnet printed, the heading gives the order to King James. Westcott (1905) says the rules "were drawn up probably under the direction of Bancroft." So "Bancroft's rules" is a probable attribution, not what the document says.
- **Tradition / hearsay:** Pollard (1911) reports that "another Bishop, Bancroft of London, is said to have insisted on fourteen alterations" to the finished text. Pollard doesn't name his source in the passage checked, and "is said to" is as strong as this claim gets.
- **Established:** Bancroft objected to the new translation at Hampton Court and prompted the ban on notes (Barlow 1604). Both come from Barlow, and the DNB account of the retort is based on Barlow too, so they aren't independent witnesses.
