# The KJV's afterlife: He/She Bibles, Wicked Bible, 1762/1769 standard texts, idiom influence

## The question

(a) The 1611 "He" and "She" Bibles: the Ruth 3:15 reading, and which printing is which.
(b) The Wicked Bible of 1631: the error, the printers (Barker and Lucas), the penalty, surviving copies.
(c) The 1769 Oxford standard text edited by Benjamin Blayney, and the 1762 Cambridge edition of F. S. Paris.
(d) Documented influence on the English language: David Crystal's *Begat* count of idioms (the figure and his method).

## Headline findings (for the node author)

1. **He/She (Established):** the first issue of the 1611 folio reads "he went into the citie" at Ruth 3:15 (the "Great He Bible"). The second issue, also dated 1611, and later editions read "she went" (the "She Bible"). Two library sources say so (Newberry, Queens' College), and a third (Garrett) cites Shaheen, *Notes and Queries* 2009. **One nuance matters:** the received Hebrew (Masoretic) text itself reads the masculine form, so "he" is arguably a faithful reading of the Hebrew, not a simple typo. Frame it as a *variant*, not just an "error." Scrivener (1884) dealt with the priority question at length, and his own conclusion needs a print check.
2. **Wicked Bible (mostly Established, with Contested details):** the octavo of 1631 prints the seventh commandment as "Thou shalt commit adultery." **Get the imprint right:** it reads "Robert Barker and the assigns of John Bill" (Princeton, Museum of the Bible). Barker and **Martin Lucas** are the King's Printers named in the proceedings (Scrivener, Folger curator, Chris Jones). The fine totalled **£300**. Four things are contested:
   - **The court.** The earliest account (Heylyn, 1668) and Scrivener say the **High Commission**. The popular story says the **Star Chamber**, and even Princeton's label gives it only as "It is said."
   - **Whether the fine was paid.** Scrivener says it was to fund Greek type. Jones says "He never pays it."
   - **The date of the proceedings.** Heylyn's account is bracketed [1632]. Scrivener dates the book itself to 1632, against a 1631 imprint.
   - **The census of surviving copies.** It has risen over time: "ten or more" (2004), 10 (2015), 11 (2024), then "17 copies in institutional hands" plus 4 to 8 private ones (Chris Jones, University of Canterbury, undated podcast).
3. **1762/1769 (Established, with one conflict):** Cambridge 1762 (printed by Joseph Bentham, sold by Benjamin Dod) and Oxford 1769 (printed by T. Wright and W. Gill) finished the standardisation, and the 1769 Oxford text is "the text we know today" (Norton/CUP; Williams in *Themelios*). **Conflict:** Scrivener says a fire in Dod's warehouse destroyed much of **Paris's 1762** impression. The DNB (1885) says a fire at the Bible Warehouse, Paternoster Row, destroyed much of **Blayney's 1769** impression. Do not merge these into one story without the print sources.
4. **Crystal (Established as Crystal's finding):** **257** idioms. His method, in his own words: he read the KJV looking for "every instance of an expression that I thought was current in modern English," then read it again. He says there is "no magic in that figure." **Only 18** of the 257 are in a form unique to the KJV. The rest go back to earlier translations (Quinion; Mlinko in *The Nation*; *Times Higher Education*). The finer breakdown circulating online (7 / 37 / 196 / 160 Geneva) could **not** be verified from a fetched source and needs the book's appendix.
