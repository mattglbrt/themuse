Hermeticism is the name for what grew up around the Hermetica, a set of late-antique writings credited to Hermes Trismegistus and long believed to be much older than they are. The Muse stops here because this is the door out of the King James trail and into the next one. Fair warning: this page is partial on purpose. It says only what the research for the KJV trail has verified, which is what the texts are, how they reached the Renaissance, how Isaac Casaubon attacked their antiquity in 1614, and that historians now argue about whether a "Hermetic tradition" existed at all. The full trail comes later.

## Historical Context

**The texts.** "The Hermetica are a body of theological-philosophical texts written in late antiquity, but long believed to be much older. Their supposed author, Hermes Trismegistus, was thought to be a contemporary of Moses" (Copenhaver, *Hermetica*, 1992, publisher's description). More on the texts themselves at [[corpus-hermeticum]].

**The Renaissance transmission.** [[marsilio-ficino]] finished a Latin translation in 1463 (Hanegraaff, 2013). It was printed at Treviso on 18 December 1471 as *Pimander seu De potestate et sapientia Dei*, "Aus dem Griech. übers. von Marsilius Ficinus" (*Gesamtkatalog der Wiegendrucke*, GW 12310).

**The wider moment.** Magic, as distinct from these texts, had a long run after Ficino. Copenhaver: "in the late fifteenth century, magic enjoyed a remarkable rebirth, acquiring the energy that kept it at the center of cultural attention for nearly two hundred years, as great philosophers and prominent naturalists tried to understand or confirm or reject it." And it ended: "Eventually, however, as Europe's most creative thinkers lost confidence in it, magic became even more disreputable than it had been before Ficino revived it" (Copenhaver, "Magic," *Cambridge History of Science*, 2006, chapter abstract). The King James Bible was translated inside that span. That's a statement about Europe and about magic, not a claim that England in 1604–1611 was a high point of anything Hermetic.

**The 1614 attack.** Three years after the King James Bible, [[isaac-casaubon]] printed his *De rebus sacris et ecclesiasticis exercitationes XVI* in London, an answer to Cardinal Baronius. Section X of its first *Exercitatio* (pp. 70–87 of the 1614 edition) argues that the book circulating under the name of Mercurius Trismegistus is pseudepigraphal, "Christiani alicuius, vel vt dicam melius, semichristiani merum figmentum" (p. 75). In a working translation made for this research, not yet checked by a Latinist, that's "the mere fiction of some Christian, or, to put it better, of a half-Christian." He argues it was put together from Plato and Scripture, that it mentions things from much later times, that its Greek is too late, and that it was written in Greek, not translated from Egyptian. He places its writer in "primis illis seculis nascentis Christianismi" (p. 77), those first centuries of nascent Christianity, and gives no year in the pages read. The full argument is on [[isaac-casaubon]] and [[corpus-hermeticum]].

## The Idea

What the Hermetica actually teach isn't covered on this page yet. Nothing here should be read as a summary of their doctrine.

What can be said is how the modern idea of "Hermeticism" was built. [[frances-yates]]'s *Giordano Bruno and the Hermetic Tradition* (1964) placed [[giordano-bruno]] "in the Hermetic tradition", in the words of her publisher's description. J. B. Trapp's British Academy memoir says that book and her *Art of Memory* "have had a lasting effect on the study of the European Renaissance". Hanegraaff, below, traces the idea of an important Renaissance "Hermetic Tradition" mainly to Kristeller and Yates.

## Claims & Disputes

**Contested: was there a "Hermetic tradition"?** Wouter Hanegraaff argues there wasn't, in any strong sense. His 2015 article says "it has been widely assumed that an important 'Hermetic Tradition' emerged during the Renaissance", traces that assumption mainly to Kristeller (1938) and Yates (1964), and says "these assumptions need to be revised." His conclusion: "we seem to be dealing with a discourse about Hermes, but hardly a 'tradition'" (*Aries*, 2015, abstract; the abstract was rebuilt from an index and needs checking against the published text).

**Contested: were the Hermetica about magic?** Brian Copenhaver, who translated them, says the ones Ficino put into Latin weren't: "But those Hermetic writings, the ones that Ficino translated, aren't about magic, and Ficino understood that. He says so in his writings" (interview, 2021). He also says Pico's *Conclusions* had no time for a Hermetic tradition: "As for a tradition called 'Hermetic', the Conclusions had nothing good to say" (*SEP*, "Pico"). These are two independent scholars, Hanegraaff and Copenhaver, reaching compatible conclusions by different routes. None of this makes Yates a straw man. Hanegraaff's own larger argument is the backdrop against which her work counted as pioneering: that these subjects were pushed out of the universities ("Expelled from the academy on the basis of Protestant and Enlightenment polemics, these traditions have come to be perceived as the Other by which academics define their identity to the present day," *Esotericism and the Academy*, 2012, publisher's description). See [[yates-thesis]].

**Established: 1614 was a serious blow, not the first doubt.** Paolo Scarpi says the polemic between Casaubon and Baronius "a donné un coup très grave à l'hermétisme", dealt Hermetism a very serious blow (*Annuaire de l'EPHE*, 2012). Florian Audureau says Casaubon proved in 1614 that these were late texts, formerly taken for the ancient wisdom of the *prisca theologia*, and did it "À la suite de plusieurs philologues du xvie siècle", following several sixteenth-century philologists (*Revue de l'histoire des religions*, 2019). That sentence doesn't name them. Modern scholars also date the texts after Christ, to the second and third centuries AD (Van den Kerchove, 2014), though Scarpi calls precise dating "problématique". Whether Casaubon was right that a Christian wrote them is a question none of the sources checked for this page rules on, so the page leaves it open.

**Dee.** [[john-dee]] reaches this topic through Yates's framework, in which he's a "Christian Cabalist" (her chapter title), not through any Hermetic text he's documented as reading in the sources checked. See [[christian-cabala]] for what is documented.
