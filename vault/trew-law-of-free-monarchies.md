*The Trew Law of Free Monarchies* is James VI's treatise on kingship, from 1598, five years before he came to the English throne. The Muse stops here because of one sentence in it. James denies that subjects may lawfully "shake off" even a wicked king, and six years later, at the [[hampton-court-conference|Hampton Court Conference]], he attacked the [[geneva-bible|Geneva Bible]] for a note that "alloweth disobedience to Kings." This page's coverage is short: one passage has been checked so far.

## Historical Context

In January 1604 James told the Hampton Court Conference that he had found in the Geneva notes "some notes very partiall, vntrue, seditious, and sauouring too much, of daungerous, and trayterous conceites," and named two, Exod. 1:19 and 2 Chron. 15:16 (Barlow 1604). *Trew Law* is his own statement of kingship from before that meeting, which is why it belongs on this trail.

## The Document

The text quoted here is from C. H. McIlwain's edition, *The Political Works of James I*, reprinted from the edition of 1616 (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1918), as put online by the Perseus Digital Library. A scan of the 1918 book is free on the Internet Archive.

## Contents

The passage that matters for this trail:

> "a wicked king is sent by God for a curse to his people, and a plague for their sinnes: but that it is lawfull to them to shake off that curse at their owne hand, which God hath laid on them, that I deny" (McIlwain 1918, Perseus text 1999.03.0071, section 4, subsection 2)

## Claims & Disputes

- **Established:** what James wrote. He denies that it is "lawfull" for subjects "to shake off that curse at their owne hand" (McIlwain 1918).
- **Analysis, not a finding:** the link to the Geneva notes. Set beside James's complaint that the note at Exod. 1:19 "alloweth disobedience to Kings" and that the note at 2 Chron. 15:16 "taxeth Asa for deposing his mother, onely, and not killing her" (Barlow 1604), this passage looks like the reason the notes offended him. That connection is this page's reading of the two texts together. No source quoted here states it.
- **Contested:** whether the Geneva notes were in fact seditious. Tom Furniss (2009) argues that "its politics are undecidable": "While some of the Geneva notes and prefaces encourage a revolutionary response to tyrants, there are many others that recommend obedience or passive resistance" (abstract).
