John Dee served Queen Elizabeth, wrote a book he said was expounded "Cabalisticè", and spent years recording conversations with angels through a scryer. In June 1604 he asked [[james-i|James]] to try him and clear his name of being a "Conjurer". No answer from the king is recorded. The Muse stops here because that petition is a documented meeting, in this trail's sources, between learned magic and the court of the king whose name is on the Bible, and because the modern argument about how seriously to take that magic runs straight through him.

## Historical Context

Dee lived in the period when, in Brian Copenhaver's words, magic kept "at the center of cultural attention for nearly two hundred years" after its late fifteenth-century revival, "as great philosophers and prominent naturalists tried to understand or confirm or reject it" (Copenhaver, "Magic," 2006, chapter abstract). That is a statement about Europe as a whole, and about learned magic in general.

In England the record for this page is thin but specific. Dee was taken into Elizabeth's service at her accession, and in 1595 he got the wardenship of Manchester College (DNB). Under James, the king himself had already published, in 1597, a treatise arguing that "such assaultes of Sathan are most certainly practized, & that the instrumentes thereof, merits most severly to be punished" ([[daemonologie]], 1597 preface, Project Gutenberg transcription).

## Life & Work

**Under Elizabeth.** The *Dictionary of National Biography* (1885–1900 series) says: "On the accession of Elizabeth, Dee was taken into the queen's service." He "eventually obtained a grant of the wardenship of Manchester College. His patent passed the great seal on 25 May 1595."

**The *Monas Hieroglyphica*.** Dee's book of 1564 was printed at Antwerp and reprinted in 1584 and at Frankfurt in 1591. The DNB gives its title as "'Monas Hieroglyphica, Mathematicè, Magicè, Cabalisticè, Anagogicèque explicata, ad Sapientissimum Romanorum, Bohemiæ, et Hungariæ regem, Maximilianum'". So the book announces itself as explained mathematically, magically, cabalistically, and anagogically, and it is addressed to Maximilian as king of the Romans, Bohemia, and Hungary. In the sources checked for this trail, Dee is the only documented route by which Christian Cabala reaches England, and this title is the plainest evidence of it. What the book argues inside is not covered yet; the title is all this page can vouch for.

**The angel conversations.** The DNB says that "Unluckily Dee soon afterwards became acquainted with Edward Kelly, alias Talbot", and that "Dee now devoted all his attention to alchemical experiments, and to a pretended intercourse with angels or evil spirits." That "pretended" is the Victorian DNB's own judgment, not a finding. Modern scholarship takes the sessions seriously as a subject: Deborah Harkness's Cambridge monograph, *John Dee's Conversations with Angels: Cabala, Alchemy, and the End of Nature* (1999), is introduced on its publisher's page this way: "John Dee's angel conversations have been an enigmatic facet of Elizabethan England's most famous natural philosopher's life and work." One of its chapters (pp. 157–194) is titled "The True Cabala" (in quotation marks), subtitled "Reading the Book of Nature". Her argument itself hasn't been read for this page.

**The 1604 petition to the king.** Dee's petition survives as a printed single sheet, *To the Kings most excellent Maiestie*, "Printed by E. Short, dwelling on Bred-streete hill neere to the end of old Fish-streete, at the signe of the Starre" (Internet Archive record of the Early English Books microfilm scan). Henry Ellis transcribed the British Museum copy for the Camden Society in 1843, under this heading:

> "The true Copie of M. John Dee his Petition to the Kings most excellent Majestie exhibited: Anno 1604 Junij 5, at Greenewich." (Ellis 1843)

Charlotte Fell Smith's 1909 biography gives the same day and place: "On June 5 he presented to James, in the Palace at Greenwich, a petition".

The charge Dee wanted cleared is set out in the petition itself: "That he is, or hath bin a Conjurer, or Caller, or Invocator of divels" (Ellis 1843; the OCR of the broadside scan has the same words). He complains that a printed work had called him "the Conjurer belonging to the most Honorable Privie Counsell" of Queen Elizabeth, and says he "hath published in Print divers his earnest Apologies against it". He asks to be tried and cleared in the king's own presence, or before the Privy Council, or before "the present assembled Parlament States". And he offers to die if the charge is proved: by stoning, by being buried alive, or "to be burned unmercifully" (all Ellis 1843). Ellis modernised u/v and i/j, so the original spelling needs the scan image.

No answer from the king is recorded. The Victorian reference books say James refused on Robert Cecil's information, but that story goes back to a single hedged account of 1707, and it's taken up under Claims & Disputes below.

**The verse appeal to the Commons.** Dee printed a second, separate broadside that month: *To the honorable Assemblie of the Commons in the present Parlament*, from the same printer, E. Short. It's in verse, it protests the same "conjurer" slander, and Dee signs it as the king's servant and mathematician. *Athenae Cantabrigienses* and Fell Smith both date it 8 June 1604, three days after the petition to the king. Fell Smith says it begged the Commons to pass "an Act Generall against slander, with a special penal order for John Dee, his case." That wording rests on her alone. Keep this document apart from the petition to the king, which asks in its own text for trial before Parliament as one of three options.

**Leaving Manchester.** The DNB: "In November 1604 Dee, being in a very weak state of health, quitted Manchester, and returned with his family to Mortlake". Smith's 1707 life gives the same month.

**Death.** The DNB says "he died in December 1608, and was buried in the chancel of Mortlake Church." The exact date is disputed, and the modern ODNB hasn't been checked, so treat December 1608 as the DNB's date, not a settled one.

## Claims & Disputes

**Contested: that James refused the petition on Cecil's information.** The old DNB (1885–1900) says: "The king, having received information from the Earl of Salisbury as to the nature of Dee's studies, refused to grant the prayer of the petition." *Athenae Cantabrigienses* (vol. 2) has nearly the same sentence. Thompson Cooper co-wrote both, so they count as one witness, not two. The earliest source found for the story is Thomas Smith's Latin life of Dee, printed in 1707, about a century after the event. Smith says, in paraphrase, that the king was informed about Dee's life and studies by Robert Cecil, whom he styles Earl of Salisbury and Treasurer of England, and "ut videtur" ("as it seems") despised him and judged him unworthy of royal favour. Smith hedges, and the passage cites no document. He also gives Cecil a title Cecil didn't yet have: Cecil became Viscount Cranborne on 20 August 1604 and Earl of Salisbury on 4 May 1605 (DNB, "Cecil, Robert"; Fell Smith 1909). On the other side, two later writers report no refusal at all. Ellis (1843): "It probably failed to attract the notice he expected". Fell Smith (1909): "Nor did James respond in any way to his heartbroken petition." Both are inferring too; no primary record of the king's answer has been found. So the record supports this much: Dee petitioned, and nothing from the king survives. The refusal on Cecil's word is Smith's 1707 account, carried into the Victorian reference books.

**Speculation: the petitions and the Witchcraft Act.** Charlotte Fell Smith (1909) connects Dee's two June appeals with the new Witchcraft Act going through Parliament that spring. She is open that it's her guess: "In what way Dee felt himself specially involved ... it is hard to conjecture". The Act's dates haven't been checked against the parliamentary record for this page, so none are given here. For now the link is her suggestion, and nothing more.

**Established: Dee has chapters in two of Yates's books.** Frances Yates's *The Occult Philosophy in the Elizabethan Age* (1979) has a chapter titled "JOHN DEE: CHRISTIAN CABALIST". Her *The Rosicrucian Enlightenment* (1972) has one titled "JOHN DEE AND THE RISE OF 'CHRISTIAN ROSENCREUTZ'", in a book whose chapters run from "A ROYAL WEDDING" through "THE ROSICRUCIAN MANIFESTOS" to "FROM THE INVISIBLE COLLEGE TO THE ROYAL SOCIETY" (chapter records, Routledge eBooks). So the picture of Dee as a Christian Cabalist, and as a figure behind the Rosicrucian story, is Yates's framing, and it should be attributed to her.

**Contested: the framework Yates put him in.** Yates's publishers present her England book as showing that occultism "had a profound impact on the rise of science in the seventeenth century" (Routledge publisher's page). That larger claim is contested. Wouter Hanegraaff argues that the idea of an important Renaissance "Hermetic Tradition" rests mainly on Kristeller (1938) and Yates (1964), and that "these assumptions need to be revised": "we seem to be dealing with a discourse about Hermes, but hardly a 'tradition'" (*Aries*, 2015). Brian Copenhaver argues that the Hermetic writings Ficino translated "aren't about magic" (interview, 2021). Neither of those sources is about Dee specifically, and no source in hand yet critiques Yates's chapter on him directly. So the fair statement is this: Dee's documented interest in Cabala is real (the *Monas* title, the Harkness book), while the grand tradition Yates set him in is under heavy revision.

**Not found: a link between Dee and the King James Bible.** Nothing in the sources checked connects Dee to the translation. He was not a translator, and the one documented contact between Dee and the new king is the 1604 petition, which drew no recorded answer.

## Where the Muse Leads

- [[james-i]]: the king Dee petitioned in 1604 to be tried and cleared of being a conjurer. No answer from him is recorded.
- [[daemonologie]]: the same king had already put in print, in 1597, that Satan's instruments "merits most severly to be punished". Worth holding next to a man asking that king to clear him of calling up devils, without claiming one explains the other.
- [[christian-cabala]]: the *Monas* calls itself explained "Cabalisticè", and Harkness reads his angel work under the heading of Cabala.
- [[yates-thesis]]: Yates gave Dee chapters in two of her books, so the argument over her thesis is partly an argument over how to read him.
- [[frances-yates]]: she wrote him up as a "Christian Cabalist" and tied him to the rise of "Christian Rosencreutz".
- [[rosicrucian-manifestos]]: Yates's chapter title puts Dee at the start of the Rosicrucian story. That's her argument, and it's worth testing on the channel.
- [[hermeticism]]: Dee reaches the Hermetic question through Yates's framework, not through any Hermetic text he's documented here as reading.
