Lancelot Andrewes (1555-1626), then Dean of Westminster, headed the first Westminster company of King James translators, which took Genesis through 2 Kings. He ended his career as Bishop of Winchester. The Muse stops here because he is the one documented bridge between the King James translators and the world of Isaac Casaubon: Andrewes was Casaubon's closest friend in England and did more than anyone to bring him there. That bridge is a friendship. Nothing in the sources checked ties Andrewes, or any other translator, to Hermetic or Cabalist learning.

## Historical Context

The KJV was made while learned magic still had the serious attention of Europe's philosophers. Brian Copenhaver writes that from the late fifteenth century magic "enjoyed a remarkable rebirth, acquiring the energy that kept it at the center of cultural attention for nearly two hundred years, as great philosophers and prominent naturalists tried to understand or confirm or reject it." (Copenhaver, "Magic," *Cambridge History of Science*, 2006, chapter abstract.)

In England, John Dee and Robert Fludd were active in these years, though neither was a translator. On 5 June 1604 [[john-dee|John Dee]] petitioned James to be cleared of being a "conjurer, or caller, or invocator of divels" (old DNB). No answer from the king is recorded. The DNB's report that he refused rests on Thomas Smith's hedged Latin life of Dee (1707) and is contested. [[robert-fludd|Robert Fludd]] became a fellow of the College of Physicians in 1609 and later published defences of the Rosicrucian fraternity at Leiden in 1616 and 1617 (old DNB).

Casaubon came to England in 1610. The old DNB records that "On 20 July 1610 an official invitation came to him from the Archbishop of Canterbury (Bancroft)," and James Zetzel, reviewing the modern edition of his English letters, writes: "In October 1610 at the invitation of James I of England, Isaac Casaubon (1559-1614), the greatest Hellenist of his age, emigrated from Paris to London."

## Life & Work

**Birth.** Andrewes was born in 1555. The Victorian DNB heads its article "ANDREWES, LANCELOT (1555–1626)", and the modern *Oxford DNB* entry by P. E. McCullough carries the same dates in its title. His friend and amanuensis Henry Isaacson, whose Life of Andrewes was first printed in 1650, gives no birth year, but says he died "in the seventy-first year of his age," which fits a birth in 1555 or 1556. He was born in the City of London, in the parish of All Hallows, Barking. Isaacson has it as "born in the city of London, in the parish of All-Saints, Barking, of honest and religious parents" (DNB; Venn; Isaacson).

**Cambridge.** He went to Merchant Taylors' School and then, in the DNB's words, "proceeded to Pembroke Hall, Cambridge, as one of Dr. Watts's scholars." Venn's *Alumni Cantabrigienses* has him matriculating at Michaelmas 1571, with the B.A. in 1574-5 (an Old Style date, so before 25 March 1575), the M.A. in 1578, and the B.D. in 1585. He was elected fellow of Pembroke in 1576 and was Master from 1589 to 1605 (DNB; Venn).

**Westminster.** Venn records a prebend of Westminster in 1597. He became Dean in 1601: the *Fasti Ecclesiae Anglicanae* records him "Pres. by queen 3 July 1601" and installed on 4 July. He held the deanery until 1605. Westminster Abbey's own page notes that "Lancelot Andrewes was Dean of Westminster from 1601-1605 but he has no memorial in the Abbey."

**Bishop.** He held three sees in turn, and the *Fasti* gives the steps for each:

- **Chichester, 1605-1609.** Elected 16 October and consecrated 3 November 1605. The DNB says he "was persuaded with some difficulty to accept the bishopric of Chichester, and was made in the same year king's almoner." (Venn also lists him as Lord Almoner, but Venn cites the DNB, so that isn't a second witness.)
- **Ely, 1609-1619.** Elected 22 September 1609, royal assent 30 September, confirmed 6 November.
- **Winchester, 1619-1626.** Elected 3 August 1618, royal assent 9 February 1619, confirmed 25 February. The *Fasti* gives modern years; in Old Style the February dates would be written 1618-19. That is why most sources start his time at Winchester in 1619.

**Translator.** The DNB says "his name stands first in the list of divines who were appointed (1607) to make our 'authorised version' of the Bible, being one of the Westminster ten whose province was to translate the Pentateuch and the historical books from Joshua to 1 Chronicles." In the Burnet list reprinted by Pollard (1911), the first Hebrew company at Westminster opens with "Mr. Dean of Westminster," whom Pollard identifies as Lancelot Andrewes. Its books are the "Penteteuchon" and "The Story from Joshua to the first Book of Chronicles, exclusive." Cambridge University Library's 2011 exhibition confirms his role: "The first Westminster company is headed by Lancelot Andrewes, Dean of Westminster." So his leadership rests on two independent sources, the list and the exhibition. The translators' Rule 13 reads: "13. The Directors in each Company, to be the Deans of Westminster and Chester for that Place; and the King's Professors in the Hebrew or Greek in either University." That made Andrewes one of the two Westminster directors, with [[william-barlow|William Barlow]], Dean of Chester, whose name heads the list for the Westminster Greek company.

**Languages.** J. H. Overton's DNB article says Andrewes "made himself master of fifteen languages, if not more."

**Casaubon's friend.** By the time Casaubon came to England, Andrewes was Bishop of Ely. The DNB article on Casaubon says: "His special friend was Lancelot Andrewes, then bishop of Ely. Andrewes, more than any other man, had been instrumental in bringing him to England." Overton's article on Andrewes adds that "Isaac Casaubon, in his 'Ephemerides,' constantly refers to the wonderful piety and learning of the (then) Bishop of Ely." These are two DNB articles by different authors in the same reference work, so they are not fully independent. Andrewes was not Casaubon's only contact among the translators: [[andrew-downes|Andrew Downes]], of the second Cambridge company, wrote to him in Greek (Cambridge University Library, 2011).

Casaubon's *De rebus sacris et ecclesiasticis exercitationes XVI*, written against Cardinal Baronius's *Annales*, was printed in London in 1614. Casaubon died on 12 July 1614 and was buried in Westminster Abbey (DNB). Whether and how that book dealt with the Hermetic writings is a question for [[isaac-casaubon]]; the research on it is still waiting on a check against the printed page.

**Death and burial.** Andrewes died in September 1626, most likely on the 25th (the day is disputed; see below). Where he died hasn't been found in the sources checked. He was buried at St Saviour's, Southwark, now Southwark Cathedral. Isaacson says he "lieth buried in the upper aisle of the parish church of St. Saviour in Southwark," and Westminster Abbey's page adds: "He has a fine monument in Southwark cathedral in London where he is buried."

## Claims & Disputes

- **Contested: the day he died.** The DNB says "He died 26 Sept. 1626." Four sources give 25 September instead. Isaacson, his amanuensis, writing in 1650: "He departed this life on the twenty-fifth day of September, 1626." The *Fasti*, citing Laud's diary and a Winchester ledger book: "D. 25 Sept. 1626 (Laud's Autobiography p. 84; LB XIII f. 7v)". Venn: "Died Sept. 25, 1626." Westminster Abbey: "He died on 25th September 1626." The weight of the evidence favours the 25th, and that is the date this page uses, but the DNB's 26th is on record.
- **Established (absence in the sources checked): no Hermetic or Cabalist link.** Overton's DNB article on Andrewes mentions his languages and his translation work. It says nothing about cabala, Hermes, magic, occult learning, astrology, Dee, or rabbinic learning. The same is true of the DNB articles on Rainolds and Lively, the Cambridge University Library's 2011 exhibition, and the translators' own preface. The research found no documented link between any KJV translator and Hermetic or Christian-Cabalist learning. That is an absence in those sources, not proof. The modern ODNB entries and the translators' library records have not been checked, and only three of the roughly fifty translators were looked at closely.
- **What the friendship does and doesn't show.** The record puts Andrewes close to Casaubon, a Hellenist he helped bring to England. It does not show Andrewes studying Hermetic or Cabalist texts, and it does not show that anything Casaubon wrote shaped the 1611 translation, which was printed three years before Casaubon's book.
