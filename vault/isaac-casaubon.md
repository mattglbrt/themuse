Isaac Casaubon was, in one reviewer's phrase, "the greatest Hellenist of his age". He moved from Paris to London in 1610 at the invitation of [[james-i|James I]], and his closest friend in England was [[lancelot-andrewes]], one of the King James translators. The Muse stops here for two reasons. Casaubon is the one documented bridge between the translators and the wider learned world this trail keeps brushing against. It's a friendship, not a source for the translation. And his big book of 1614, printed three years after the 1611 Bible, argued that the Hermetic writings were neither Egyptian nor ancient, but the work of a Christian, or as he put it, a half-Christian, of the first Christian centuries.

## Historical Context

Casaubon came to an England where learned magic still had the serious attention of Europe's thinkers. Copenhaver describes magic after its late fifteenth-century revival as kept "at the center of cultural attention for nearly two hundred years, as great philosophers and prominent naturalists tried to understand or confirm or reject it" (Copenhaver, "Magic," 2006, chapter abstract). The Hermetic texts themselves were, in Copenhaver's words, "written in late antiquity, but long believed to be much older" (Copenhaver, *Hermetica*, 1992, publisher's description). Walter Scott, editing them in 1924, put the length of that belief at "about thirteen hundred years, from the time of Lactantius to that of Casaubon" (Scott, *Hermetica* I, 1924, Introduction).

## Life & Work

**The move to England.** The DNB says: "On 20 July 1610 an official invitation came to him from the Archbishop of Canterbury (Bancroft)." James Zetzel, reviewing the modern edition of Casaubon's English correspondence, puts it this way: "In October 1610 at the invitation of James I of England, Isaac Casaubon (1559-1614), the greatest Hellenist of his age, emigrated from Paris to London." The king, says the DNB, "granted him a pension of 300l. a year from his own purse, in addition to the prebend at Canterbury."

**Andrewes.** The DNB article on Casaubon: "His special friend was Lancelot Andrewes, then bishop of Ely. Andrewes, more than any other man, had been instrumental in bringing him to England." The DNB article on Andrewes, by a different author, adds that "Isaac Casaubon, in his 'Ephemerides,' constantly refers to the wonderful piety and learning of the (then) Bishop of Ely". Both come from the same reference work, so they aren't fully independent witnesses.

**Downes.** A second translator wrote to him too. Cambridge University Library's 2011 King James Bible exhibition says of [[andrew-downes]]: "With his pupil John Bois, Downes was a member of the second Cambridge company of Bible translators. His Greek letters to Isaac Casaubon (1559-1614), classical scholar and philologist, are in the British Library".

**The 1614 book.** Casaubon's *De rebus sacris et ecclesiasticis exercitationes XVI*, written against Cardinal Baronius's *Annales*, was printed in London in 1614. The archive.org record of the first edition reads "Isaaci Casauboni De rebus sacris et ecclesiasticis exercitationes 16. ad cardinalis Baronii Prolegomena in Annales" and "Londini : Ex officina Nortoniana apud Ioan. Billium". The DNB says the work "took up the last four years of his life, and probably hastened his death".

**Where Hermes sits in it.** The argument about Hermes is section X of the first *Exercitatio*, which begins at the foot of p. 70 of the London 1614 edition and runs to p. 87, where section XI begins. It answers Baronius's *Apparatus*, Num. XVIII (marked on p. 71). The section takes the Sibylline oracles, Hydaspes, and Hermes together. Its heading on p. 70 reads: "X. Indicatur error Baronij & aliorum, nimium tribuentium vaticinijs Sibyllarum, Hydaspis, Mercurij Trismegisti & similium." The heading names Baronius's error ("error Baronij") and lists the Sibyls, Hydaspes, and Mercurius Trismegistus together.

A note on the English below. No published English translation of these passages turned up in the research. The English renderings on this page are a working translation made for this research, not yet checked by a Latinist. The Latin is given as transcribed from the 1614 page images, with the long s printed as s. Where a modern scholar is quoted in French, the English that follows is a paraphrase.

**His verdict.** On p. 75 Casaubon says he is not afraid to declare, with every assurance, that the book circulating under the name of Mercurius Trismegistus is pseudepigraphal, being "Christiani alicuius, vel vt dicam melius, semichristiani merum figmentum": in the working translation, "the mere fiction of some Christian, or, to put it better, of a half-Christian." The section ends on p. 87:

> "Falsissimum etiam est quod docti indocti videntur hactenus credidisse; aut scripta hæc fuisse à Mercurio Trismegisto, vetustissimo Ægypti sapiente; aut ex illius scriptis esse versa. quorum vtrumque probauimus esse longe absurdissimum."

Working translation: "Most false too is what learned and unlearned alike seem to have believed until now: either that these writings were by Mercurius Trismegistus, the most ancient sage of Egypt, or that they were translated from his writings. We have proved both of these to be by far the most absurd."

**His grounds.** Summarised from the pages read, he argues on four fronts.

- *Content.* The doctrine isn't Egyptian. On p. 77 it is "partim Græcam è Platonis & Platonicorum libris, & quidem persæpe ipsis eorũ verbis, depromptam: partim Christianam è libris sacris petitam", partly Greek, from the books of Plato and the Platonists, often in their very words, and partly Christian, from the sacred books. The summary on p. 71 calls the book "è Platone & Scripturis concinnatum", stitched together from Plato and the Scriptures. On the same p. 77 he reads the name Poimandres as "Pastor", the shepherd, and says "Pœmander hic siue Pastor, Christus est, qui apud Iohannem ait", going on to quote, in Greek, John's "I am the good shepherd".
- *Anachronism.* The book mentions the sculptor Phidias and the victory of Eunomus of Locri at the Pythian games. "Pythicus vero agon Delphis multis seculis post Hermetis Ægyptij tempora institutus" (p. 86): the Pythian contest at Delphi was founded many centuries after the time of the Egyptian Hermes. His own date for that real Egyptian Mercurius, on the same page, is very ancient, "circa annum à conditu rerum bis millesimum", around the year 2000 from the creation of the world. That date is Casaubon's opinion, not history.
- *Language.* On p. 86 he says the style is utterly foreign to the Greek spoken by Hermes's supposed contemporaries, that the book uses words not known even to the Greek current around the time of Christ's birth, and "Nego etiam ex alia lingua versa hæc esse": he denies they were translated from another language.
- *The general case.* The section summary on p. 70 argues that Christian mysteries were hidden before Christ yet are set out plainly in the Mercurius book and the Sibyllines, that the old Greeks knew none of this, and that in the first times of the Church it was customary "bono fine falsos libros fingere", to forge false books for a good end. He also surveys what ancient writers said of Hermes's books, including "Galeni & Plutarchi loca, de libris iam olim falso Hermeti adscriptis" (p. 71), passages of Galen and Plutarch on books long since falsely ascribed to Hermes.

**His date for the forger.** He gives no year in the pages read. He puts the writer "primis illis seculis nascentis Christianismi" (p. 77), "in those first centuries of nascent Christianity", a time when books under false titles were forged daily, and describes him as someone lightly imbued with Christian things. He thinks the forger's aim was to show that Christian doctrines had been known to the most ancient sages. He approves the aim and condemns the method: "Laudo propositum: consilium vehementer improbo" (p. 75), I praise the purpose, I strongly disapprove of the plan.

**Death.** "He died at last of an injudicious trip to Greenwich on 12 July 1614. He was buried in Westminster Abbey" (DNB). The day may differ between sources that count by the old and new calendars; that hasn't been resolved.

## Claims & Disputes

**Established: he wasn't the first to doubt Hermes.** Florian Audureau credits Casaubon with proving, in 1614, that these were late texts, formerly taken for the ancient wisdom of the *prisca theologia*, but he says Casaubon did it following earlier scholars: "À la suite de plusieurs philologues du xvie siècle, Isaac Casaubon réussit à prouver en 1614 qu'il s'agissait de textes tardifs alors qu'on les avait tenus pour la révélation de la prisca theologia des Anciens" (*Revue de l'histoire des religions*, 2019). Audureau doesn't name those sixteenth-century philologists in that sentence. So his book came into an argument that was already going. Paolo Scarpi calls the polemic between Casaubon and Baronius "un coup très grave à l'hermétisme", a very serious blow to Hermetism (*Annuaire de l'EPHE*, 2012).

**Established in outline, contested in detail: his late dating.** Modern scholars also date the Hermetica after Christ. Anna Van den Kerchove describes the Corpus Hermeticum as seventeen Greek texts "écrits au cours du iie et du iiie siècle", written in the second and third centuries AD, and dates the Poimandres, one of the oldest, between the late first and mid-second century (*Revue de l'histoire des religions*, 2014). Scott in 1924 put most or all of them in the third century, and "probably none so early as the first century". Scarpi adds that their dating "demeure encore problématique et très difficile à déterminer", remains problematic and very hard to pin down.

**Unresolved on this page: his Christian author.** Van den Kerchove says Casaubon's reading of Poimandres as John's shepherd "est lié à son idée que les textes hermétiques ont été écrits par des chrétiens", is tied to his idea that the Hermetic texts were written by Christians. Her own description of the Corpus is of texts offering "une voie de sagesse mêlant traditions grecque et égyptienne", a path of wisdom mixing Greek and Egyptian traditions. None of the sources checked for this page gives a verdict on Casaubon's claim of Christian authorship, so the page doesn't give one either.

**Not found: any translator drawing on Hermetic learning.** Andrewes brought Casaubon over and Downes wrote to him, but nothing in the sources checked (the DNB articles on Andrewes, Rainolds, and Lively, the Cambridge 2011 exhibition, and the 1611 preface) mentions Hermes, cabala, magic, or occult learning. That's an absence in the sources checked, not proof of a negative. Either way, this friendship shouldn't be read as a Hermetic influence on the Bible. The 1614 book came three years after 1611, in the year Casaubon died, and it argued against the Hermetic writings' antiquity.

## Where the Muse Leads

- [[lancelot-andrewes]]: the translator who, more than any other man, brought Casaubon to England. This is the documented bridge.
- [[andrew-downes]]: a second translator, whose Greek letters to Casaubon survive in the British Library.
- [[james-i]]: the king who invited him and paid him £300 a year from his own purse.
- [[richard-bancroft]]: the archbishop whose official invitation of July 1610 brought him over.
- [[corpus-hermeticum]]: the texts he took apart in 1614, arguing from Plato, Scripture, anachronism, and language that they were neither Egyptian nor ancient.
- [[hermeticism]]: Scarpi says the 1614 polemic dealt Hermetism a very serious blow, three years after the King James Bible.
- [[marsilio-ficino]]: his Latin, printed in 1471 "as Hermes Trismegistus' book on the Power and Wisdom of God" (Hanegraaff), is the attribution Casaubon set out to break.
