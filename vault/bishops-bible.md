The Bishops' Bible is the English Bible, first issued in 1568, that the King James translators were ordered to follow. The first of their rules reads: "The ordinary Bible read in the Church, commonly called the Bishops Bible, to be followed, and as little altered as the Truth of the original will permit." The Muse stops here because the familiar KJV wording began, by rule, as a revision of this book, and one of the copies the translators marked up still survives in Oxford.

## Historical Context

The research behind this page does not yet cover the Bishops' Bible's own making in the 1560s. What it does cover is its standing by 1604: it was "the ordinary Bible read in the Church," in the words of the translators' first rule.

It also made a stronger claim on its title page than the KJV later did. A. W. Pollard (1911) notes that Bishops' Bibles printed between 1585 and 1602 described themselves as authorised and appointed, and that no one had found an order to back that wording either: "As far as I know it has never been contended that there was any Order in Council passed in 1584 or 1585 to justify this, and it seems therefore far from safe to postulate the existence of such an Order in 1611." The 1611 KJV title page reads "Appointed to be read in Churches" and does not use the word "authorized."

## The Document

**The rules.** The fifteen rules for the translators survive through Gilbert Burnet's *History of the Reformation*, reprinted by Pollard (1911). Rule 1 makes the Bishops' Bible the base. Rule 14 names the translations to fall back on "when they agree better with the text than the Bishops' Bible: Tyndale's, Coverdale's, Matthew's [Rogers'], Whitchurch's [Cranmer's], Geneva." (Hall 1881 transcription; the copies of the rules differ in wording.) Who drew the rules up is disputed: Burnet's heading reads "An Order set down for the Translating of the Bible, by- King James," while Westcott (1905) says they "were drawn up probably under the direction of Bancroft." Cambridge University Library's 2011 exhibition sums up the arrangement: "The Bishops' Bible was to be the base text, with Tyndale's, Matthew's and Coverdale's Bibles, the Great Bible and the Geneva Bible used to correct it when necessary."

**The translators' copies.** The Bodleian Library's record for its copy Arch. A b.18 says that "forty unbound copies of the 1602 edition of the Bishops' Bible were distributed to the translators to serve as the basis for their translation." The Bodleian copy is a folio printed by [[robert-barker|Robert Barker]], and its annotations "reflect the work-in-progress of four of the six translating companies." The record also quotes a description of it as "a large Bible wherein is written downe all the Alterations of the last Translacion." (Bodleian IIIF manifest, catalogue and exhibition caption; the annotations themselves were not read for this page.)

**Reading the annotations.** Timothy Berg (2023), citing E. C. Jacobs's 1972 dissertation, reports the sigla in the margins: Jacobs "correctly identified "g" as the Geneva Bible, the KJB's famous predecessor. Other scholars have suggested that "t" is for Tremellius and "j" is for Junius or Jerome's Vulgate." The "t" and "j" readings are suggestions, and Berg is a single, non-scholarly source.

**What the delegates said.** The English delegates' report to the Synod of Dort (1618; printed 1620) describes the brief in the same spirit as Rule 1. Their account, as Westcott prints it, says the old version long received by the Church was to be purged of faults, and a new one was not simply to be made: "Prime, cautum est vt simpliciter nova versio non adomaretur, sed vetus, & ab Ecclesia diu recepta ab omnibus naevis & vitiis purgaretur" ("adomaretur" is OCR, probably for "adornaretur"). The report does not name the Bishops' Bible in that sentence.

## Contents

**Psalm 46.** The 1568 text already has both words the Shakespeare myth depends on: "though the mountaynes shake at the surges of the same. Selah" … "& knappeth the speare in sunder" (1568 text, textusreceptusbibles.com transcription, checked against Franssen's independent count). Franssen puts them at these positions: "the word 'shake' is in forty−seventh position and the word 'speare' in forty−eighth counting from the end."

## Claims & Disputes

- **Speculation: Shakespeare hid his name in Psalm 46.** The claim, as Franssen states it: "in this forty−sixth Psalm the forty−sixth word from the beginning should be 'shake,' the forty−sixth word from the end 'speare'". The earliest printed version Franssen found is Anthony Burgess's Shakespeare biography (1970); William Harmon, who put it forward as a tentative suggestion in *Connotations*, says "I cannot remember when, where, or how I first heard about Psalm 46." What the record shows: both words were already in the Bishops' Bible of 1568, the text the translators were told to follow, and Franssen notes that "already in Coverdale's translation we find the corresponding words to be 'shoke' and 'speare.'" His verdict: "In the absence of the least shred of corroborative evidence, Shakespeare's involvement in or authorship of part of the Authorized Version seems extremely unlikely." The full count, and how it depends on skipping the superscription and the last "Selah," belongs on [[king-james-bible]].
- **Contested: who wrote the rules.** Burnet's heading credits King James. Westcott thought Bancroft "probably" directed them. Calling them "Bancroft's rules" is a convention, not something the document says.
