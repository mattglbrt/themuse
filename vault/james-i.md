James was King James VI of Scotland, and from 1603 king in England too, the James whose name the [[king-james-bible]] carries. The Muse stops here because the same man stands at both ends of the first trail. In 1590 and 1591 he sat in on the examinations of accused witches. In 1597 he published a book arguing that witchcraft is real and should be punished. In 1604 he called for a new English Bible, and that same year John Dee petitioned him to be tried and cleared of being a conjurer. No answer from the king is recorded. None of that needs embroidering. The documents set it out, and this page follows them.

## Historical Context

James was born in Edinburgh Castle in 1566. The old *Dictionary of National Biography* puts it this way: "JAMES VI (1566–1625), king of Scotland, afterwards James I, king of England, son of Henry Stuart, lord Darnley, and Mary Queen of Scots, was born on 19 June 1566, in Edinburgh Castle" (Gardiner, DNB vol. 29). A contemporary Scottish chronicle, the *Diurnal of Occurrents*, and Westminster Abbey give the same day and place. He was king of Scots almost from the cradle: "On 24 July 1567 he became king by his mother's enforced abdication, and was crowned at Stirling on 29 July" (DNB). The *Diurnal* also records the coronation at Stirling on 29 July 1567. For the 24 July date the DNB is the only non-tertiary source checked.

The record here opens in Scotland in 1590 and 1591, with the witch hunt now named for North Berwick. The standard scholarly edition of its documents describes James as "alleged victim, interrogator, judge, and demonologist" in that hunt (Normand & Roberts 2000, abstract). Six years later his *Daemonologie* was printed by "Robert Walde-graue, / Printer to the Kings Majestie. An. 1597." (1597 title page, Gutenberg #25929). His *Trew Law of Free Monarchies* followed in 1598.

In 1603 he came to the English throne. The DNB: "At last, on 24 March 1603, Elizabeth died, and James was at once proclaimed in England by the title of James I, king of England, though he subsequently styled himself, without parliamentary authority, king of Great Britain." The printed proclamation, from Robert Barker's press, dates Elizabeth's death "March, 24. Anno Domini, 1602," (raw OCR of the Early English Books scan). That is not a mistake. England counted the new year from 25 March, so 24 March was still the last day of 1602 there. Scotland had already changed. David Masson, editing the Scottish Privy Council register, explains: "Till 1752 the 25th of March continued to be, for most purposes of dating, the first day of the New Year in England, while in Scotland the New Year had been counted from the Ist of January, as by the present ordinance, ever since 1600" (*Register of the Privy Council of Scotland*, vol. VI, 1884, introduction). The ordinance he means is dated 17 December 1599 (Masson; single source for that date). So the same day is 24 March 1602 in an English document and 24 March 1603 in a Scottish one. The DNB says James "left Edinburgh for his new kingdom on 5 April" (single source).

The Puritan clergy "took advantage of the accession of a new monarch in 1603 to present James I with a petition," and "James responded by summoning the Hampton Court conference" (Lockyer, *Oxford Companion to British History*). The petition "was presented to James on his way to London after his accession. The date is April, 1603." (Gee & Hardy 1896, headnote). The same year, *Daemonologie* was reprinted twice in London (STC 14365 and 14366, per the Open Library and Wellcome catalogue records).

His English reign runs through the making of the 1611 Bible. In the same years [[john-dee|John Dee]] petitioned him (1604), and [[robert-fludd|Robert Fludd]] addressed a manuscript to him and dedicated a book to him (1617-24), though for Fludd the only source so far is the old *Dictionary of National Biography*. Neither man was a translator. The one documented link between the translators and that world of learning runs through [[isaac-casaubon|Isaac Casaubon]], below.

## Life & Work

### The Danish marriage, 1589-90

James married Anne of Denmark first by proxy. The DNB says "the marriage was celebrated by proxy at Copenhagen on 20 Aug. 1589," and A. W. Ward's DNB article on Anne gives the same date. The contemporary chroniclers split on the month. An annalist the 1828 Bannatyne Club editor identifies with George Marjoribanks' *Annals* puts it in July ("quhilk wes donne in the mounthe of Julay therafter"). An anonymous contemporary historian in the same volume puts it in August ("In the moneth of Auguft of the nixt yeir 1589"; the OCR reads the long s as "f"). So the month is disputed. The DNB goes with August.

Anne's fleet didn't reach Scotland that year. The same annalist says it sailed in September and was driven "by contrarious vind and tempeft of wether" to Norway. The DNB: "The young queen was, however, driven by a storm to Norway, and James, impatient of delay, set sail from Leith on 22 Oct. to see what had become of her." The anonymous historian also gives 22 October, and a royal declaration on the government of the realm in his absence is dated "At Leith, the xxij day of October." Marjoribanks' annalist disagrees and puts the sailing on the "penult" (second-last) day of October. The weight of the sources is with 22 October, but the variant is there. One account printed in the 1828 volume has James land at "Slaikray" in Norway on 28 October and reach Oslo on 19 November (single source).

They married in person at Oslo. The DNB: "He found her at Opslo, near the site of the modern Christiania, where the pair were married on 23 Nov." David Moysie's contemporary *Memoirs* agree: "Upon the 23 day of November 1589, the King and Queine wer maried in Upslo, be Mr David Lyndsay, minister at Leith." The next day's grant of Dunfermline to Anne as her morning gift is dated "AT UPSLO, 24 NOV. 1589."

They spent the winter in Denmark, where, in Marjoribanks' words, they "marriet of new." Westminster Abbey's page dates a ceremony at Kronborg to 21 January 1590 (single source for that date; the Abbey page doesn't mention the Oslo wedding at all). The DNB: "on 21 April 1590 James and his queen sailed for Scotland, landing at Leith on 1 May." Moysie and Marjoribanks both confirm the landing on 1 May 1590. Anne was crowned in the abbey kirk at Holyrood on 17 May 1590, according to Marjoribanks' annalist and the 1590 account of the coronation printed in the same volume.

That voyage matters here because the North Berwick documents keep coming back to it. *Newes from Scotland* has Agnes Sampson repeat to James the words he and Anne spoke "at Upslo in Norway the first night of their mariage," and it reports a confession that a christened cat caused a contrary wind for the king's ship "at his comming foorth of Denmarke." Both stories are below.

### The king in the room: North Berwick, 1590-91

The London pamphlet [[newes-from-scotland|Newes from Scotland]] says on its title page that it prints "the true examination of the saide doctor and witches, as they vttered them in the presence of the Scottish king." Its account of [[agnes-sampson|Agnes Sampson]] says she "was taken and brought to Haliriud house before the Kings Maiestie and sundry other of the nobility of Scotland, where she was straitly examined" (EEBO-TCP A00710).

The pamphlet also reports how the king reacted. It says "his Maiestie saide they were all extreame lyars," and that Sampson then "declared vnto him the verye woordes which passed betweene the Kings Maiestie and his Queene at Upslo in Norway the first night of their mariage." That is the pamphlet's claim. Nobody can check what was said in a bedroom in Oslo.

Part of what the accused confessed concerned James himself. *Newes* reports that "the said christened Cat was the cause that the Kinges Maiesties Ship at his comming foorth of Denmarke, had a contrary winde to the rest of his Ships." This is confession content, and it should be read as confession content. *Newes* also names the torture used on the accused: the pilliwinks on [[geillis-duncan|Geillis Duncane]]'s fingers, and "the bootes" on [[john-fian|John Fian]]. Normand and Roberts call the pamphlet "the most propagandastic of all the texts generated by the North Berwick witch trials" (sic).

The scholarly database confirms the king's part. The University of Edinburgh's *Survey of Scottish Witchcraft* lists "James (VI) Stewart" among the investigators in Sampson's case and records: "She confessed to these things in front of King James VI." It gives her trial on 21 January 1591, a guilty verdict, and execution on Castle Hill on 28 January 1591, "Strangle & Burn" (Survey, case C/EGD/63 and trial T/LA/934). Fian was tried on 26 December 1590 and found guilty (trial T/LA/928). More on the hunt is at [[north-berwick-witch-trials]].

### Daemonologie, 1597

[[daemonologie|Daemonologie]] is a dialogue in three books. In his preface James says the first speaks "of Magie in general, and Necromancie in special. The second of Sorcerie and Witch-craft: and the thirde, conteines a discourse of all these kindes of spirits, & Spectres" (Gutenberg #25929, preface).

The preface gives his reason for writing:

> "The fearefull aboundinge at this time in this countrie, of these detestable slaues of the Deuill, the Witches or enchaunters, hath moved me (beloued reader) to dispatch in post, this following treatise of mine"

He wrote it, he says, "not in any wise (as I protest) to serue for a shew of my learning & ingine, but onely (mooued of conscience) to preasse thereby, so farre as I can, to resolue the doubting harts of many" ... "both that such assaultes of Sathan are most certainly practized, & that the instrumentes thereof, merits most severly to be punished" (Gutenberg #25929, preface).

He names two opponents. [[reginald-scot|Reginald Scot]], "the one called SCOT an Englishman, is not ashamed in publike print to deny, that ther can be such a thing as Witch-craft." [[johann-weyer|Johann Weyer]]: "The other called VVIERVS, a German Phisition, sets out a publick apologie for al these craftes-folkes" (same).

### The Trew Law of Free Monarchies, 1598

In [[trew-law-of-free-monarchies|The Trew Law of Free Monarchies]] James argues that subjects may not throw off even a bad king:

> "a wicked king is sent by God for a curse to his people, and a plague for their sinnes: but that it is lawfull to them to shake off that curse at their owne hand, which God hath laid on them, that I deny"

(*Trew Law*, in McIlwain (ed.), *The Political Works of James I*, 1918, via Perseus. This is the only copy of the passage checked so far.)

### Burntisland, 1601

Before Hampton Court there was Burntisland, where the Kirk of Scotland talked about mending its Bible, with the king in the room. The General Assembly met at Burntisland on 12 May 1601, and the register records the king and his commissioners present (*Booke of the Universall Kirk*, Part III, 1839, p. 963). David Calderwood's *History* says it had been called for St Andrews but was moved "becaus the king fell at the hunting, and hurt his left shoulder" (vol. VI, p. 105).

In the last session, on 16 May, the register takes up the translation. Here it is as the archive.org OCR of the 1839 Bannatyne Club edition reads it. The OCR renders the long s as "f", so "fundrie" is "sundrie" and "tranflatioun" is "translatioun":

> "It being meanit be fundrie of the brethren, that there was fundrie errours that merited to be correctit in the vulgar tranflatioun of the Byble, and of the Pfalmes in meetter ; as alfo that ther are fundrie prayers in the Pfalme Booke quhilk wald be altered"

(*Booke of the Universall Kirk*, Part III, p. 970.) Calderwood gives the same item in plain type: "In the last sessioun it was meaned by sindrie of the brethrein, that there were sindrie errours in the vulgar translatioun of the Bible, and of the Psalmes in meter, which required correcting" (vol. VI, p. 124). Both credit the motion to some of the brethren, not to James. As the church historian William Craig puts it, "The official record says nothing more, nor does it report any comment from King James, who was present at the meeting" (Craig 2011). The record calls the text only the "vulgar translatioun" and doesn't say which Bible that was.

The Assembly resolved three things. Ministers with the best knowledge of the languages were to work on the parts of the vulgar translation that needed mending and compare their work at the Assembly. Robert Pont, minister at St Cuthbert's, was to revise the metrical Psalms, and his work was to be reviewed at the next Assembly. The prayers were left alone: "It is not thocht good, that the prayers alreadie contenit in the Psalme Booke be altered or delatit," though new ones could be added once the Assembly had tried and allowed them (British History Online transcription of the 1839 edition).

Nothing came of it in Scotland. John Spottiswoode's *History of the Church of Scotland* ends the episode: "but nothing was done in the one or the other" (Spottiswoode Society edition, vol. III, p. 99). Craig reports no mention of it in the records of the following Assembly. Whether James himself pushed for it is a separate question, and it rests on Spottiswoode alone (see Claims & Disputes).

### Hampton Court, 1604

The [[millenary-petition|Millenary Petition]] of April 1603 offered to settle the Puritans' grievances "by conference among the learned" (Gee & Hardy 1896). James called that conference at Hampton Court. The main record of it is [[william-barlow|William Barlow]]'s [[summe-and-substance-of-the-conference|Summe and Substance of the Conference]] (London, 1604), which dates the conference "Ianuary 14. 1603," Old Style, so January 1604. Barlow was a partisan reporter (see Claims & Disputes). What follows is what Barlow says James said.

On the second day, [[john-rainolds|John Rainolds]] ("D. Reynalds the Foreman" of the four Puritan speakers) "moued his Maiestie, that there might bee a newe translation of the Bible, because, those which were allowed in the raignes of Henrie the eight, and Edward the sixt, were corrupt and not aunswerable to the truth of the Originall." [[richard-bancroft|Richard Bancroft]], Bishop of London, objected "that if euery mans humour should be followed, there would be no ende of translating."

James sided with the proposal. Barlow's text, from the 1604 edition (EEBO-TCP A04434):

> "VVhereupon his Highnesse wished, that some especiall paines should be taken in that behalfe for one vniforme translation (professing that hee could neuer, yet, see a Bible well translated in English; but the worst of all, his Maiestie thought the Geneua to bee)"

He laid out a process: "this to bee done by the best learned in both the Vniuersities, after them to bee reuiewed by the Bishops, and the chiefe learned of the Church; from them to bee presented to the Priuie-Councell; and lastly to bee ratified by his Royall authoritie; and so this whole Church to be bound vnto it, and none other."

Then came the ban on notes. "Marry, withall, hee gaue this caueat (vpon a word cast out by my Lord of London) that no marginall notes should be added." So the ban was prompted by Bancroft, the man who had just opposed the translation. James gave his reason in the same passage:

> "hauing found in them, which are annexed to the Geneua translation (which he sawe in a Bible giuen him by an English Lady) some notes very partiall, vntrue, seditious, and sauouring too much, of daungerous, and trayterous conceites"

He cited two examples: "Exod. 1. 19. where the marginall note alloweth disobedience to Kings. And 2. Chron. 15. 16. the note taxeth Asa for deposing his mother, onely, and not killing her" (Barlow 1604). The 1804 reprint of Barlow gives the same passage in modernised spelling. Which edition of the [[geneva-bible|Geneva Bible]] James had seen is not known. Barlow says only that an English lady gave it to him.

Put that beside the *Trew Law* passage above and you can see why a note allowing "disobedience to Kings" would bother him. That link is this page's reading of the two texts. No source checked for this page makes it in so many words.

The translators' own preface of 1611, [[translators-to-the-reader|The Translators to the Reader]], tells the story less kindly to the Puritans. It says the conference was appointed "upon the importunate petitions of the Puritanes, at this Majesties comming to this Crowne," and that the Puritans raised the translation as a fallback when their other grounds failed, calling the existing Bible "a most corrupted translation." Even so, the preface says, "yet even hereupon did his Majestie beginne to bethinke himselfe of the good that might ensue by a new translation" (Wikisource transcription of the 1611 text).

### The Bible of 1611

By the summer of 1604 the translators were being chosen. In a letter of 22 July 1604 the King spoke of "certain learned men to the number of four and fifty for the translating of the Bible" (quoted in Pollard 1911). The names of only about fifty survive, and no single list has more than 47. Bancroft sent out the lists of the six companies that summer (Cambridge University Library, 2011).

The translators' rules survive in Burnet's *History of the Reformation*, whose heading assigns the "Order" to King James (Pollard 1911, reprinting Burnet). Rule 1 made the [[bishops-bible|Bishops' Bible]] the base text. Rule 6 allowed "No Marginal Notes at all to be affixed, but only for the Explanation of the Hebrew or Greek Words," which is James's Hampton Court caveat in working form. Rule 9 names the king's interest outright: each finished book was to go to the other companies "to be consider'd of seriously and judiciously, for His Majesty is very careful in this Point."

The 1611 title page says the translation was revised by his Majesty's special commandment (paraphrased; the Pollard transcription is garbled at that word), and ends: "Appointed to be read in Churches. Imprinted at London by Robert Barker, Printer to the Kings most Excellent Maiestie. Anno Dom. 1611." [[robert-barker|Robert Barker]] was the King's Printer. The engraved title page by Cornelis Boel shows "all twelve Apostles plus Moses, Aaron and the Holy Trinity (but not King James)" (CUL 2011). James appears in the dedication that comes before the preface, which Pollard's OCR transcribes as "To the most High and Mightie Prince, lames by the grace of God King of Great Britaine, France and Ireland, Defender of the Faith, &c." ("lames" is the OCR's reading of "Iames"; single source).

Whether the king, or anyone, ever formally "authorized" the book is a separate question, under Claims & Disputes.

### A conjurer's petition, a scholar pensioned

**John Dee, 1604.** Dee's petition to the king survives as a printed broadside of 1604, *To the Kings most excellent Maiestie*, "Printed by E. Short, dwelling on Bred-streete hill" (Internet Archive record of the Early English Books scan). Henry Ellis printed the British Museum copy in 1843 under the heading "The true Copie of M. John Dee his Petition to the Kings most excellent Majestie exhibited: Anno 1604 Junij 5, at Greenewich." Dee asked to be tried and cleared of the slander "That he is, or hath bin a Conjurer, or Caller, or Invocator of divels" (Ellis 1843; the broadside's own OCR has the same words, garbled). He wanted the trial held "eyther in your owne royall presence and hearing ; or, of the Lordes of your Majesties most honorable Privie Counsell : or, of the present assembled Parlament States," and he offered himself "willingly, to the punishment of Death" if the charge were proved, by stoning, burial alive, or burning. He also pointed out that he had "published in Print divers his earnest Apologies against it" (Ellis 1843).

No record of the king's answer has been found. The old DNB says James refused the petition on information from the Earl of Salisbury. That story traces back to a hedged sentence in a Latin life of Dee written a century later, and it is disputed (see Claims & Disputes).

Three days later, on 8 June 1604, Dee sent a second and separate appeal, in verse, *To the honorable Assemblie of the Commons in the present Parlament*, from the same printer (*Athenae Cantabrigienses* vol. 2; Fell Smith 1909). He signed it as the king's servant and mathematician, and it protests the same "conjurer" slander. According to his biographer Charlotte Fell Smith, it asked the Commons for "an Act Generall against slander, with a special penal order for John Dee, his case" (Fell Smith only). Keep the two apart. The verse broadside went to the Commons. The petition to the king only mentions Parliament, as one of three places he'd accept a trial.

**Isaac Casaubon, 1610.** Casaubon got an official invitation from Archbishop Bancroft on 20 July 1610 (DNB), and "In October 1610 at the invitation of James I of England, Isaac Casaubon (1559-1614), the greatest Hellenist of his age, emigrated from Paris to London" (Zetzel, *BMCR* 2019). The DNB says the king "granted him a pension of 300l. a year from his own purse, in addition to the prebend at Canterbury" (DNB only). The DNB also names [[lancelot-andrewes|Lancelot Andrewes]], a translator of the new Bible, as his "special friend," who "more than any other man, had been instrumental in bringing him to England." Casaubon's *De rebus sacris et ecclesiasticis exercitationes XVI*, written against Baronius's *Annales*, was printed in London in 1614. Andrewes's friendship with Casaubon is the one documented bridge between the translators and the learned world around the king. No source checked shows any translator drawing on Hermetic or Cabalist learning.

**Robert Fludd.** The DNB records that Fludd addressed a manuscript to the king: "To a manuscript 'Declaratio breuis,' which he addressed to James I, are appended the confirmatory letters of French and German associates." His *Utriusque Cosmi … Historia* (Oppenheim and Frankfurt, 1617-24) "has two dedications, first to the Deity, secondly to James I." Fludd had publicly defended the Rosicrucian fraternity in print in 1616 and 1617 (DNB; *Catholic Encyclopedia* 1912). Both facts about Fludd and the king rest on the DNB alone.

### Death, 1625

The DNB: "On 5 March 1625 James was attacked by a tertian ague. ... James's condition varied from day to day, but on 27 March he died at Theobalds." Westminster Abbey agrees: "James died on 27th March 1625 at Theobald's Park in Hertfordshire." The 1911 *Encyclopaedia Britannica* gives "the 5th of March 1625" as the date of death. That is wrong. The 5th is the day the DNB gives for the start of his last illness.

## Claims & Disputes

**Contested (one witness): did James urge a new translation at Burntisland in 1601?** The register of the Assembly credits the motion to some of the brethren and records no word from the king, and Calderwood's *History* follows the register (see Life & Work). The case for James rests on John Spottiswoode's *History of the Church of Scotland*, first printed in London in 1655 (p. 465). In the 19th-century Spottiswoode Society edition it reads: "After this a proposition was made for a new translation of the Bible, and the correcting of the Psalms in metre. His majesty did urge it earnestly, and with many reasons did persuade the undertaking of the work, showing the necessity and the profit of it" (vol. III, p. 98). Spottiswoode says James pointed out "sundry escapes" (errors) in the common translation and "did recite whole verses of the same" Psalms to show the faults of the metre (pp. 98-99). He then ties the episode to England: "Yet did not the king let this his intention fall to the ground, but after his happy coming to the crown of England set the most learned divines of that Church a-work" (p. 99). That link is Spottiswoode's own reading, written after the fact. Later writers who give James the initiative, such as Allan Westcott (1911), who says James had "broached" the Bible undertaking at Burntisland, depend on Spottiswoode. William Craig (2011) reads James's quick uptake of Rainolds's proposal in 1604 the same way: "the way he pounced on Rainolds' suggestion suggests that he had been pondering the question at least since Burntisland." That is one historian's inference. What is established is the Assembly's resolution and that nothing came of it. That James argued for it himself has one witness.

**Contested: did James refuse Dee on Salisbury's advice?** The old DNB says: "The king, having received information from the Earl of Salisbury as to the nature of Dee's studies, refused to grant the prayer of the petition." The DNB article's author, Thompson Cooper, co-wrote *Athenae Cantabrigienses*, which says the same, so the two count as one witness. The earliest source found is Thomas Smith's Latin life of Dee, printed in 1707, about a century later. In paraphrase, Smith says the king was informed about Dee's life and studies by Robert Cecil, whom he styles Earl of Salisbury and Treasurer of England, and "ut videtur" ("as it seems") despised him. The passage cites no document. Cecil did not hold that title in June 1604. His DNB entry gives his creations "on 20 Aug. 1604 Viscount Cranborne, on 4 May 1605 Earl of Salisbury." Two other accounts record no refusal at all. Ellis (1843) thought the petition "failed to attract the notice he expected," and Fell Smith (1909) wrote: "Nor did James respond in any way to his heartbroken petition." Both of those are inferences too. No primary record of the king's answer has been found. So the petition is established. The refusal on Salisbury's information is Smith's hedged account, carried into Athenae and the DNB.

**Contested: were the Geneva notes seditious?** That James *called* them "seditious" is established, as far as Barlow's record goes. Whether they were is disputed. Tom Furniss (2009) notes that "Since its first publication in 1560, the Geneva Bible has been considered by many as a revolutionary or seditious text, especially because of the numerous explanatory notes," but argues that "its politics are undecidable": "While some of the Geneva notes and prefaces encourage a revolutionary response to tyrants, there are many others that recommend obedience or passive resistance" (*Prose Studies* 31.1, abstract). So far Furniss is the only scholar checked on the counter-position.

**Contested: how far to trust Barlow.** Everything James says at Hampton Court on this page comes through Barlow, Dean of Chester, who wrote the semi-official account. The church historian William Craig reports that Mark Curtis (1961) called the *Summe and Substance* "a skilful piece of party propaganda," mainly because of where it differs from an anonymous Harleian account (Craig, blog, 2008). Frederick Shriver (1982) revisited the conference and calls it "one of the most significant events in the political and religious history of England." Treat the king's words here as what Barlow printed, not a transcript.

**Contested: who wrote the translators' rules.** Burnet's heading attributes the "Order" to King James. Westcott (1905) says the rules "were drawn up probably under the direction of Bancroft." Calling them "Bancroft's rules" is a probable attribution, and the document does not say it.

**Established, with a historical counter-view: was it ever "authorized"?** The title page says "Appointed to be read in Churches." It does not say "authorized." Westcott: "no evidence has yet been produced to shew that the version was ever publicly sanctioned by Convocation or by Parliament, or by the Privy Council, or by the king." Pollard (1911): "no authority has ever been discovered for the words 'Appointed to be read in Churches' which appear on the title-page of all editions, nor for the phrase, the 'Authorized Version'." The counter-view is Lord Selborne's, as Pollard quotes it: "Nothing in my opinion, is less likely than that the King's printer should have taken upon himself … to issue the book … with a title-page asserting that it was 'Appointed to be read in Churches ' if the fact were not really so." Selborne (1881) suggested an Order in Council, noting that the Council registers for 1600 to 1613 were destroyed by fire. Pollard answers that the Bishops' Bibles of 1585 to 1602 carried "Authorised and Appointed" with no known Order behind them, so "it seems therefore far from safe to postulate the existence of such an Order in 1611."

**Speculation: the king handed the manuscript to Francis Bacon.** William T. Smedley, in *The Mystery of Francis Bacon* (1912), wrote that "It will eventually be proved that the whole scheme of the Authorised Version of the Bible was Francis Bacon's." His case leans on the king: "It was carried out under the personal supervision of the King, but every record of the proceedings has disappeared," and "In i6og the translators handed their work to the King, and in 1610 he returned it to them completed" (OCR "i6og" is 1609). Smedley gives no source for that "curious statement" in the passage retrieved. Manly P. Hall's *Secret Teachings of All Ages* (1928) repeats it, quoting Smedley: "King James turned over to him the translators' manuscripts." The claim still circulates, for instance in a 2019 video titled "King James Bible Final Edit and ReWrite by Freemason Founder, SIR Francis Bacon." The record points the other way. Pollard's 1911 collection prints a translator list, the rules, payments, and a reviser's contemporary account. Anthony Walker's life of John Bois says the final revisers "went dayly to Stationers Hall, & in three quarters of a year, finished their task," and that Bois "took notes of their proceedings." Ward Allen (1969), who edited those notes, concludes that the revisers "must, to all intents and purposes, have been solely responsible for the composition of the final version." Allen does not discuss Bacon, and no source checked puts Bacon in the record.

## Where the Muse Leads

- [[king-james-bible]]: the Bible that carries his name, ordered at the conference he called, and printed by his printer.
- [[daemonologie]]: his own book on witches, written after he had watched the accused examined.
- [[north-berwick-witch-trials]]: the hunt where, in the scholars' words, he was victim, interrogator, judge, and demonologist.
- [[newes-from-scotland]]: the pamphlet that puts him in the room at Holyrood with Agnes Sampson, and brings up his wedding night at Oslo.
- [[agnes-sampson]]: she confessed in front of him, and was strangled and burned on Castle Hill.
- [[hampton-court-conference]]: where he called for "one vniforme translation," and where Spottiswoode's story of Burntisland says the idea ended up.
- [[summe-and-substance-of-the-conference]]: Barlow's partisan record, the source for nearly every word James says on this page.
- [[trew-law-of-free-monarchies]]: his theory of kingship, the obvious thing to read beside his complaint about the Geneva notes.
- [[geneva-bible]]: the Bible he called "the worst of all," and whose notes he called seditious.
- [[john-dee]]: petitioned him to be cleared of being a conjurer the same year he ordered the new Bible, and got no recorded answer.
- [[isaac-casaubon]]: the scholar he invited and pensioned, and a friend of the translator Andrewes.
- [[robert-fludd]]: the Rosicrucian apologist who dedicated his great book to James, second only to God.
