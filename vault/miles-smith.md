Miles Smith (died 1624) was one of the King James translators, a member of the Oxford company that worked on the Prophets. The English delegates to the Synod of Dort reported that he and Bishop Thomas Bilson gave the translation its final touches, and Pollard and Westcott name him as the writer of the unsigned preface, [[translators-to-the-reader|The Translators to the Reader]]. He was made Bishop of Gloucester in 1612. The Muse stops here because, if the attribution holds, the voice that speaks for all the translators at the front of the 1611 Bible is his.

## Historical Context

The translators were organised into six companies. Cambridge University Library's 2011 exhibition puts it simply: "Six companies of divines and scholars were formed, two each in Westminster, Oxford and Cambridge. Each was allocated a different portion of the Bible to translate." The English delegates to the Synod of Dort gave the same division in 1618, independently.

The standard list of who sat where is the one Bishop Burnet printed "Ex MS. D. Borlase," reprinted by Pollard in 1911, who calls it "the most trustworthy" of the lists that survive. In it, the first Oxford company (Hebrew) is "Doctor Harding Dr. Reynolds Dr. Holland Dr. Kilbye Mr. Smith Mr. Brett Mr. Fairclough," and its books are "The four, or greater Prophets, with the - Lamentations, and the twelve lesser Prophets." (Raw OCR of Pollard's reprint.) The "Dr. Reynolds" on that list is [[john-rainolds|John Rainolds]], who had proposed the new translation at Hampton Court. The first name, John Harding, was Regius Professor of Hebrew and President of Magdalen. This rests on the one list; Fuller's independent list of 1655 has not yet been checked.

## Life & Work

**Hereford.** His birth date isn't known. Edward Irving Carlyle's DNB article is headed "SMITH, MILES (d. 1624), bishop of Gloucester," and Joseph Foster's *Alumni Oxonienses* gives no birth date either. Both say he came from Hereford. The DNB calls him "son of a butcher," and Foster calls him "'a fletcher's son'" (see below).

**Oxford.** The DNB says he "became, about 1568, a student of Corpus Christi College, Oxford, from which college he migrated to Brasenose." Foster gives the degree dates: "B.A. 31 Jan., 1572-3, M.A. 27 June, 1576, B.D. 1 Feb., 1584-5," and "D.D. from Brasenose Coll. 4 July, 1594". The B.A. and B.D. dates are Old Style, so by the modern year they fall in 1573 and 1585, which is how the DNB gives them.

**Canon.** The DNB: "in 1580 he obtained the prebend of Hinton in Hereford cathedral." Foster adds "canon of Exeter 1595-9" and "fellow of Chelsea College 1610" (the Chelsea fellowship is in Foster only).

**Translator.** The DNB sums up his part: he "took part in the translation of the prophetic books, but he and Thomas Bilson [q. v.], bishop of Winchester, were appointed to make a final revision of the text of the Old Testament, and to Smith was assigned the honour of writing the preface to the completed work". Pollard's note on the Oxford list identifies the "Mr. Smith" there: "Miles Smith, of Brasenose ... one of the two final revisers of the version of 1611, and the writer of the preface; made Bishop of Gloucester, 1612." (Pollard 1911, leaf 66.)

**Final reviser.** After the companies finished, the text went through a review stage whose size is disputed (see below). Then, on the Dort delegates' account as Pollard summarises it, "the final touches to the translation were given by Bilson, Bishop of Winchester, and Miles Smith, afterwards Bishop of Gloucester." (Pollard 1911, leaf 73.) Pollard's sentence comes from the Dort report, so the two are one witness, not two.

**The preface.** "The Translators to the Reader" is unsigned in the 1611 text. Pollard calls Smith "the writer of the preface," and Westcott (1905) describes it as "Dr Miles Smith, afterwards Bishop of Gloucester, writing in the name of his fellow-labourers." See [[translators-to-the-reader]].

**Bishop of Gloucester, 1612-1624.** Both old reference works say the bishopric was his reward for the translation. The DNB: "As a reward for his labour he was appointed bishop of Gloucester". Foster, quoting an older source: "bishop of Gloucester 1612-24 'for his great pains in translating the bible,'". The DNB says he was "consecrated at Croydon on 20 Sept. 1612." The online transcription of the modern *Fasti Ecclesiae Anglicanae* dates this entry differently, but that text contradicts itself and is waiting on a check against the printed volume, so this page follows the DNB and Foster.

**Death.** He died on 20 October 1624. The DNB: "Smith died on 20 Oct. 1624". The *Fasti* agrees: "See vac. by his d., from 20 Oct. 1624". Foster says he was "buried in Gloucester cathedral 9 Nov., 1624" (the burial date is from Foster only).

## Claims & Disputes

- **Contested: his father's trade.** The DNB says "son of a butcher." Foster says "'a fletcher's son'" (a fletcher made arrows), and prints the phrase in quotation marks with the reference "Ath. ii. 359". The two haven't been reconciled.
- **Established, as an attribution: the preface is his.** The DNB, Pollard, and Westcott all name him. None of them says what the attribution rests on, and the preface itself is unsigned.
- **Contested: how big the review board was.** John Bois's early biography says a board of six, "two out of every company," reviewed the whole work. The Dort report says twelve: "ex hisce omnibus duodecim selecti viri in unum locum convocati integrum opus recognoverunt et recensuerunt," and Pollard also speaks of "the final board of twelve." The two accounts don't agree.
- **Contested: how much room there was for a final touch.** Ward Allen (1969) dated Bois's notes on the revision to 1610-11 and concluded that "the company of revisers must, to all intents and purposes, have been solely responsible for the composition of the final version of the Authorized Version, for this computation leaves almost no time for Bishop Bilson and Dr. Smith's finishing touch." So the Dort report says Bilson and Smith finished the text, and Allen's dating leaves them almost no time to do it.
- **Speculation: Francis Bacon, not the translators, finished the KJV.** The earliest source the research found is William T. Smedley, *The Mystery of Francis Bacon* (1912): "It will eventually be proved that the whole scheme of the Authorised Version of the Bible was Francis Bacon's." Part of his case is a story for which the passage read gives no source: "And now a curious statement has come down to us. In i6og the translators handed their work to the King, and in 1610 he returned it to them completed." (OCR "i6og" is 1609.) Manly P. Hall repeated the claim in *The Secret Teachings of All Ages* (1928), quoting Smedley, and it still circulates online. What the record shows: Anthony Walker's life of John Bois says the revisers "went dayly to Stationers Hall, & in three quarters of a year, finished their task," and that Bois alone kept a record: "he & he only, took notes of their proceedings." Those notes survive (Corpus Christi College, Oxford, MS 312), and Allen's study of them, quoted above, credits the revisers with the final text. No source the research found puts Bacon in the record.
