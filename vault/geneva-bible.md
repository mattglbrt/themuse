The Geneva Bible is the English Bible whose margins King James could not stand. Many readers have treated its notes as seditious since it first appeared in 1560, and at the Hampton Court Conference in January 1604 James said it was the worst English translation he had seen and called some of its notes "seditious." The Muse stops here because the new Bible James ordered that day was to carry no such notes, and yet the Geneva stayed on the short list of older translations the KJV translators were told to check their work against.

## Historical Context

Tom Furniss opens his 2009 study of the notes with the book's reputation: "Since its first publication in 1560, the Geneva Bible has been considered by many as a revolutionary or seditious text, especially because of the numerous explanatory notes that the translators added in the margins of the text." (Furniss 2009, abstract.)

Its most famous critic was James. At the [[hampton-court-conference|Hampton Court Conference]] (January 1604; the title of William Barlow's account gives the Old Style year, 1603), the puritans' spokesman [[john-rainolds|John Rainolds]] "moued his Maiestie, that there might bee a newe translation of the Bible." According to Barlow's semi-official account, James answered:

> "VVhereupon his Highnesse wished, that some especiall paines should be taken in that behalfe for one vniforme translation (professing that hee could neuer, yet, see a Bible well translated in English; but the worst of all, his Maiestie thought the Geneua to bee)"

Barlow then records the rule about notes, and who prompted it: "Marry, withall, hee gaue this caueat (vpon a word cast out by my Lord of London) that no marginall notes should be added." The "Lord of London" was [[richard-bancroft|Richard Bancroft]], bishop of London since 1597 (DNB). James gave his reason:

> "hauing found in them, which are annexed to the Geneua translation (which he sawe in a Bible giuen him by an English Lady) some notes very partiall, vntrue, seditious, and sauouring too much, of daungerous, and trayterous conceites"

(All Barlow quotations: *The Summe and Substance of the Conference*, 1604, EEBO-TCP A04434. The 1804 reprint on archive.org gives the same passage.)

James had set out his own view of kingship six years earlier, in [[trew-law-of-free-monarchies|The Trew Law of Free Monarchies]] (1598): "a wicked king is sent by God for a curse to his people, and a plague for their sinnes: but that it is lawfull to them to shake off that curse at their owne hand, which God hath laid on them, that I deny" (McIlwain ed., 1918). Setting that passage beside the notes James objected to is this page's reading. No source consulted here makes the connection in those words.

## The Document

The research behind this page covers the Geneva Bible mostly from the outside, through James's objection and the KJV's use of it. What it says about the book itself is short.

- **Which copy James saw is unknown.** Barlow says only that it was "a Bible giuen him by an English Lady." No edition is named.
- **Its debt to Tyndale.** Ronald Mansbridge (1995) counted the words in nine sample chapters and found that "more than 83% of the words in the Geneva Version were taken direct from Tyndale, and more than 81% of the words in the King James Version." That is one scholar's count, and estimates of Tyndale's share are disputed (see [[william-tyndale]]).
- **Its place in the making of the KJV.** The translators' rules made the Bishops' Bible the base text, and Rule 14 named the older translations to use "when they agree better with the text than the Bishops' Bible: Tyndale's, Coverdale's, Matthew's [Rogers'], Whitchurch's [Cranmer's], Geneva." (Hall 1881 transcription; the surviving copies of the rules differ in wording.) Cambridge University Library's 2011 exhibition puts it the same way: "The Bishops' Bible was to be the base text, with Tyndale's, Matthew's and Coverdale's Bibles, the Great Bible and the Geneva Bible used to correct it when necessary."
- **Traces in the translators' working copy.** The translators' annotated 1602 Bishops' Bible survives at the Bodleian. Timothy Berg, who cites E. C. Jacobs's 1972 dissertation, writes that Jacobs "correctly identified "g" as the Geneva Bible, the KJB's famous predecessor." (Berg 2023; a seminary-institute blog, single source.)
- **How much the KJV owes it.** James Carleton (1902) argued that the Catholic Rheims New Testament mattered nearly as much: "yet with respect to the distinctive touches which the Authorized New Testament has derived from the earlier translations, her debt to Roman Catholic Rheims is hardly inferior to her debt to puritan Geneva." That is Carleton's judgment.

## Contents

**The notes James named.** Barlow gives his two examples: "As for example, Exod. 1. 19. where the marginall note alloweth disobedience to Kings. And 2. Chron. 15. 16. the note taxeth Asa for deposing his mother, onely, and not killing her." Those are James's descriptions of the notes, as Barlow printed them. The notes' own wording in the 1560 and 1599 editions has not yet been checked against a facsimile, so this page doesn't quote them.

**Psalm 46.** Paul Franssen, answering the Shakespeare-in-Psalm-46 claim, counted the Geneva text: "the forty−seventh word is 'shake,' the forty−fourth word from the end is 'speare.'" (Franssen 1993/94; the Geneva text itself was not fetched for this page.) The myth is covered on [[bishops-bible]] and [[king-james-bible]].

## Claims & Disputes

- **Contested: were the notes seditious?** That James said so is established, on Barlow's record. Whether the notes were seditious is disputed. Furniss argues that "its politics are undecidable" and that "While some of the Geneva notes and prefaces encourage a revolutionary response to tyrants, there are many others that recommend obedience or passive resistance." So far the research has one scholar for this counter-position.
- **Contested: how far to trust Barlow.** Barlow, then Dean of Chester, was a partisan reporter. The church historian William Craig reports that Mark Curtis (1961) called it "a skilful piece of party propaganda": "The reasons Curtis gave to substantiate his claim that The Summe and Substance is unreliable and 'a skilful piece of party propaganda' almost all come from points where it differs from this Harleian Account." (Craig 2008.) James's words on this page are what Barlow printed, not a transcript.

- [[hampton-court-conference]]: where James called the Geneva the worst English Bible and attacked its notes.
- [[summe-and-substance-of-the-conference]]: Barlow's record, the only source here for James's exact words about the notes.
- [[james-i]]: the king whose objection to the notes is on record.
- [[trew-law-of-free-monarchies]]: James's own case against subjects throwing off a wicked king, written six years before he complained about the notes.
- [[richard-bancroft]]: the "Lord of London" whose word prompted the ban on marginal notes.
- [[king-james-bible]]: the translation made without Geneva's kind of notes, with Geneva still on its list of aids.
