The Millenary Petition is the request for church reform that puritan ministers presented to [[james-i|James I]] when he came to the English throne in 1603. It takes its name from the "more than a thousand" ministers it claims to speak for. The Muse stops here because the petition asked for the matter to be settled "by conference among the learned," and James answered with the [[hampton-court-conference|Hampton Court Conference]], where the [[king-james-bible]] was first proposed.

## Historical Context

Henry Gee and William John Hardy, whose 1896 collection prints the text, say in their headnote: "THIS petition was presented to James on his way to London after his accession. The date is April, 1603." An encyclopedia article on the conference hosted by the Christian Classics Ethereal Library agrees: "presented to James while he was on the way to London in Apr., 1603." That article probably draws on the same older histories as Gee and Hardy, so it may not be an independent check.

Roger Lockyer, in the *Oxford Companion to British History*, describes the moment this way: "They took advantage of the accession of a new monarch in 1603 to present James I with a petition, said to have 1,000 signatories, setting out their position. James responded by summoning the Hampton Court conference and using the millenary petition as its agenda."

## The Document

No manuscript stands behind the text used here. Gee and Hardy transcribed it from Thomas Fuller's *Church-History* of 1655: "Transcr. Fuller's Church History, edit. 1655, book x. p. 21" (Gee & Hardy 1896, headnote). So the chain runs Fuller (1655), then Gee and Hardy (1896), then the online transcription by the Hanover Historical Texts Project.

Who carried it to the king is thinly documented. The old *Dictionary of National Biography* calls Arthur Hildersam "one of the most active managers of the so-called 'millenary' petition for church reforms" (DNB, "Hildersam, Arthur," 1891).

## Contents

The petitioners open by saying who they are and who they aren't. They write as "we, to the number of more than a thousand of your majesty's subjects and ministers, all groaning as under a common burden of human rites and ceremonies," and they come "neither as factious men affecting a popular parity in the Church, nor as schismatics aiming at the dissolution of the State ecclesiastical, but as the faithful servants of Christ and loyal subjects to your majesty" (Gee & Hardy 1896).

The requests fall under headings on the Church service, ministers, livings and maintenance, and discipline. Under the Church service they ask "that the cross in baptism, interrogatories ministered to infants, confirmation, as superfluous, may be taken away; baptism not to be ministered by women" ... and "the cap and surplice not urged; that examination may go before the communion; that it be ministered with a sermon" (Gee & Hardy 1896).

Near the end comes the line that led to Hampton Court. They offer to be heard further, "if it shall please your highness further to hear us, or more at large by writing to be informed, or by conference among the learned to be resolved" (Gee & Hardy 1896).

They sign off as "Your majesty's most humble subjects, the ministers of the Gospel that desire not a disorderly innovation, but a due and godly reformation."

## Claims & Disputes

- **Contested (the date):** Gee and Hardy date the petition April 1603, presented on James's journey south, and the CCEL encyclopedia article agrees. The DNB article on Arthur Hildersam says instead that the petition was "presented at Hampton Court in January 1604." The two can't both be right. This page follows April 1603, which has two reference works behind it, and treats the DNB date as an error or a conflation of the petition with the conference.
- **Contested (the number):** "more than a thousand" is the petition's own claim, and Lockyer is careful to say only that it was "said to have 1,000 signatories." The number was already in question by 1896: Gee and Hardy note that "The question of the number of the signatories is discussed by Gardiner, Hist. Eng., i. 148, note." What Gardiner concluded hasn't been checked for this page.
- **Contested (framing):** Lockyer says James used the petition "as its agenda" at Hampton Court. How closely the conference followed the petition isn't settled here.
