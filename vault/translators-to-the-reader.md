"The Translators to the Reader" is the preface printed at the front of the 1611 King James Bible. It is unsigned, and scholars attribute it to [[miles-smith|Miles Smith]]. The Muse stops here because it is the translators' own account of what they thought they were doing, what they worked from, and what they consulted. When a claim about the KJV's making needs checking, this is the first primary text to check it against.

## Historical Context

The preface gives its own version of how the project started, and it is not a neutral one. It says the [[hampton-court-conference|Hampton Court Conference]] was called on puritan petitions, and that the new translation came out of it anyway:

> "the very Historicall trueth is, that upon the importunate petitions of the Puritanes, at this Majesties comming to this Crowne, the Conference at Hampton Court having bene appointed for hearing their complaints" ... "a most corrupted translation." ... "yet even hereupon did his Majestie beginne to bethinke himselfe of the good that might ensue by a new translation."

Barlow's account of the conference credits [[john-rainolds|John Rainolds]] with proposing the translation. The preface frames the puritans' request less kindly than Barlow does. Both are partisan.

## The Document

**Where it sits.** Pollard (1911) describes the 1611 preliminaries in order: the dedication to [[james-i|King James]], then the preface "headed ' The Translators to the Reader '", then a calendar.

**The heading and the author.** The preface is headed "THE TRANSLATORS TO THE READER" and names no author in the text. Pollard calls Miles Smith "the writer of the preface," and Westcott (1905) describes the preface as "Dr Miles Smith, afterwards Bishop of Gloucester, writing in the name of his fellow-labourers." Neither says what the attribution rests on.

**The text used here.** Quotations come from the Wikisource transcription of a 1611 scan, in original spelling. A second transcription, from the Oxford 1833 exact reprint in modernised spelling, is at Bible Research. Neither has been checked against a 1611 facsimile for this page, and the long-s may have been normalised.

## Contents

**What they worked from.** "If you aske what they had before them, truely it was the Hebrew text of the Olde Testament, the Greeke of the New."

**What they consulted.** "Neither did wee thinke much to consult the Translators or Commentators, Chaldee, Hebrewe, Syrian, Greeke, or Latine, no nor the Spanish, French, Italian, or Dutch;"

**On the Septuagint.** The preface says the Greek translation departs from the Hebrew in many places, while defending the Apostles' use of it: "The translation of the Seventie dissenteth from the Originall in many places, neither doeth it come neere it, for perspicuitie" ... "yet which of the Apostles did condemne it?" See [[septuagint]].

**The aim.** Revision, not replacement: "wee never thought from the beginning, that we should neede to make a new Translation, nor yet to make of a bad one a good one ... but to make a good one better, or out of many good ones, one principall good one".

**How long it took.** "the worke hath not bene hudled up in 72. dayes, but hath cost the workemen, as light as it seemeth, the paines of twise seven times seventie two dayes and more". Westcott and Pollard both work this out as about two years and nine months.

**On varying their wording.** The preface also explains why the translators did not render a given word the same way every time. (The exact passage is held for a context check before it is quoted here.)

## Claims & Disputes

- **Established, as an attribution: Miles Smith wrote it.** Pollard and Westcott both name him. The 1611 text is unsigned, and neither scholar states the evidence. Treat "by Miles Smith" as a scholarly attribution, not a signature.
- **Established (absence in the sources checked): no Cabala or Hermes.** The preface names Chaldee, Hebrew, and Syriac commentators, which is language scholarship. It does not mention Cabala, Hermes, or any named rabbi such as Kimchi. The research behind this site found no documented link between any KJV translator and Hermetic or Christian-Cabalist learning in the sources it checked: the preface, the Cambridge University Library's 2011 exhibition, and the old DNB entries on Andrewes, Rainolds, and Lively. That is an absence in those sources, not proof that no link existed.
