# Research memo: Popular myths about the King James Bible

Date: 2026-09-22 · Phase 1, question 6 (retry) · Log: `research/logs/2026-09-22-kjv-myths.log.md` · Sources: `research/sources/2026-09-22-kjv-myths.json`

## The question

(a) Shakespeare in Psalm 46: "shake" is the 46th word from the start, "spear" the 46th from the end, and Shakespeare was 46 in 1610. Check the word counts against a 1611 text. Who first circulated the claim, when, and what do scholars say?
(b) Francis Bacon as secret editor of the KJV: who makes the claim, since when, and what does the documented record of the translators show?
(c) Was the KJV ever formally "authorized"? The title-page wording "Appointed to be read in Churches", and scholarly statements on the absence of any recorded authorizing act.

## Headline answers

- **(a)** The count is **real but conditional**. In the 1611 text, "shake" is word 46 from the start **only if the superscription is skipped**, and "speare" is word 46 from the end **only if the closing "Selah" is skipped** (it is word 47 if Selah counts). Both words were already in the Bishops' Bible of 1568, at positions 47 and 48 (my count, matching Franssen's). The Geneva Bible has them at 47 and 44, and Coverdale already had "shoke" and "speare". Earliest printed statement found: **Anthony Burgess, *Shakespeare* (1970)**, per Franssen. The oral origin is **not found**: William Harmon, who published it in 1992/93, says he cannot remember where he first heard it. Gillingham (via UBS TIPs) credits its popularity to an unidentified *Times* article. Scholarly verdict (Franssen; Gillingham): Shakespeare's involvement is "extremely unlikely". **Tier: Speculation** for the authorship claim. The word positions themselves are Established (anyone can count them), with the counting conventions stated.
- **(b)** Earliest source found: **William T. Smedley, *The Mystery of Francis Bacon* (London, 1912)**: "It will eventually be proved that the whole scheme of the Authorised Version of the Bible was Francis Bacon's." Smedley's case rests on missing records, the translators' supposed lack of literary gift, and an unsourced "curious statement" that the King kept the work from 1609 to 1610. **Manly P. Hall, *The Secret Teachings of All Ages* (1928)** repeats it, quotes Smedley, and adds that the manuscripts were in Bacon's hands "for nearly a year". The claim is still circulating: a 2019 video titled "King James Bible Final Edit and ReWrite by Freemason Founder, SIR Francis Bacon" is archived on archive.org. The documented record points the other way. It includes a translator list, Bancroft's rules, and Walker's account of a final revision board at Stationers' Hall, where John Bois took notes. Bois's notes survive in Fulman's copy (Corpus Christi Oxford MS 312), and Ward Allen dates them to 1610–11. Allen concludes the revisers "must, to all intents and purposes, have been solely responsible for the composition of the final version". No source found puts Bacon in the record. **Tier: Speculation.**
- **(c)** The 1611 title page says "Appointed to be read in Churches". It does not say "authorized". Westcott (1905) says no evidence shows it was "ever publicly sanctioned by Convocation or by Parliament, or by the Privy Council, or by the king". Pollard (1911) gives the opposing view (Lord Selborne: the King's printer would not have printed that wording falsely). He rebuts it: "Appointed" is "considerably weaker than the 'Authorised and Appointed'" of the Bishops' Bibles of 1585–1602, and it is "far from safe to postulate" an Order in Council in 1611. **Tier: Established** (no recorded authorizing act), with the Selborne view noted as the historical counter-position.

## Source-quality notes

- **Primary documents.** The 1611 text (textusreceptusbibles.com transcription, cross-checked against Wright's Cambridge 1909 reprint). The 1568 Bishops' Bible text (textusreceptusbibles.com; my positions 47/48 match Franssen's independent count, which checks the transcription). Pollard 1911 prints the primary documents: the 1611 title page, Bancroft's rules and Walker's *Life of Bois*. Smedley 1912 and Hall 1928 are primary **for the claim** (the history of the idea), never for history.
- **Scholarly secondary.** Franssen and Harmon (*Connotations*, a peer-reviewed journal). Allen 1969 (Vanderbilt UP). Westcott 1905 (standard 19th-c. history, revised by W. A. Wright). Pollard 1911 (Oxford/Frowde, documentary edition with a critical introduction). Pollard's and Westcott's age means a node author may want a modern confirmation of row 27 (e.g. Daniell or Campbell). None was accessible this session; Daniell's IA copy returned 403.
- **Tertiary.** UBS TIPs relays Gillingham's words. Treat those rows as needs-print-check until Gillingham p. 172f. is checked. Folger is an authoritative institutional page, but tertiary.
- **Textusreceptusbibles.com** is a devotional parallel-text site with no stated editorial source for its transcriptions. It is only trusted here because Wright 1909 (1611) and Franssen's count (1568) confirm it.
- **The count method is this memo's own analysis, not a cited claim.** Rows 2, 4 and 6 show the fetched text, and anyone can recount. Conventions: punctuation ignored, "&" counts as a word, the superscription is excluded, and the closing "Selah" is excluded. The page should say plainly that the "46/46" result depends on those two exclusions.
- **Independence.** Hall depends on Smedley, since he quotes him. Harmon and Franssen are an exchange, so they are not independent on Burgess's priority. Row 9 is therefore single-source.
- **Not researched / open.** Smedley's and Hall's claim about a "Baconian headpiece" shared by the 1611 Bible and the 1623 Folio was not tested. If it goes on the page, it needs its own question. Kipling's story, mentioned by Franssen, was not identified or fetched.
