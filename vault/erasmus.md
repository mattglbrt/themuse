Erasmus edited the first Greek New Testament to be published, printed at Basel in 1516. The Muse stops here because the King James New Testament sits at the end of a line of printed Greek texts that starts with him, and because his first edition had a few Latin patches in it. Where his one manuscript of Revelation ran out, he translated the missing verses into Greek from the Latin Vulgate, and said so.

## Historical Context

Erasmus was not the first to print a Greek New Testament, only the first to publish one. William W. Combs: "Though the Complutensian Polyglot was printed in 1514, Erasmus' Greek NT of 1516 was the first one to be published. It was, as has been noted, a Latin-Greek edition, which he called Novum Instrumentum." It was printed at Basel by Johann Froben (Combs 1996). Lincoln H. Blumell gives the title in full: "By March 1516 the first edition of Erasmus's Greek New Testament was printed; it was entitled Novum Instrumentum Omne."

## Life & Work

**Birth and name.** The year of his birth is disputed (see Claims & Disputes), and so, in a small way, is the place. The day is given in the 1911 *Encyclopaedia Britannica*: he "was born on the night of the 27/28th of October, probably in 1466". Libraries and scholars conventionally print his dates as 1466-1536. Blumell: "Desiderius Erasmus (1466–1536), the famous Dutch humanist from Rotterdam, was ordained a Catholic priest in 1492."

**To Basel and Froben.** Blumell: "In the summer of 1514, Erasmus left for Basel, Switzerland, where he was approached by a well-known printer named Johannes Froben about the possibility of producing a Greek New Testament." The 1911 *Britannica* says that "Froben received him with open arms, and the presses were soon busy with his books." Later he moved there for good. The *Britannica* again: "in November 1521 settled permanently at Basel, in the capacity of general editor and literary adviser of Froben's press."

**Five editions of the Greek New Testament.** The first came out in 1516 as *Novum Instrumentum omne*. Cambridge University Library transcribes the imprint of its copy as "Apud ... Basilaeam: In ædibus Ioannis Frobenij. Anno. M.D.XVI [1516]" and describes the book as "A revised version of the Greek New Testament, with a Latin translation." Four more editions followed. Blumell: "In 1519 a second edition was produced in which a number of typographical emendations were made." Then: "Again in 1522 a third edition was published in which a few substantive changes were made." And: "By the time of Erasmus's fourth edition in 1527, which proved to be the definitive edition (although a fifth and final edition came out in 1535)". The 1911 *Britannica* gives the same years: "In four reprints, 1519, 1522, 1527, 1535, Erasmus gradually weeded out many of the typographical errors". The sources used here name Froben as printer of the 1516 edition only. Who printed the later four isn't established on this page.

**The manuscripts.** Combs describes how the 1516 text was put together: "Seven manuscripts were used by Erasmus in Basel to compile the Greek text" ... "Codices 2e and 2ap themselves served as the printer's copy for all the NT except Revelation." ... "For the book of Revelation, Erasmus had only one manuscript (1r)." (Combs 1996, pp. 45-46.)

**The Latin patches.** That one manuscript of Revelation was incomplete. Combs: "Because Codex 1r was missing its last page and thus the last six verses of Revelation (22:16–21), Erasmus retranslated these verses from the Latin Vulgate, and he honestly admitted in the Annotationes that he had done so." Blumell says the same: "When Erasmus provided the last six verses of Revelation, he relied on a copy of the Latin Vulgate." Combs gives a second case, Acts 9:6: the words "And he trembling and astonished said, Lord, what wilt thou have me to do? And the Lord said unto him" "were inserted by Erasmus at this point because they were in the Vulgate" (Combs 1996, p. 47). Combs adds that the Acts 9:6 words have no Greek manuscript support and stay in the later received text.

**Later editions.** The [[comma-johanneum|Comma Johanneum]] (1 John 5:7b-8a) first appeared in his third edition, of 1522 (Combs 1996, pp. 48-50). [[william-tyndale|Tyndale]] translated from his Greek. F. H. A. Scrivener, 1881: "Tyndale himself followed the second and third editions of Erasmus's Greek text (1519, 1522)."

**What came after.** Next came Stephanus, whose 1550 edition "became the accepted form of the TR, especially in England," and then Beza, whose editions the King James translators used (Combs 1996; Blumell 2019). More than a century after Erasmus, a 1633 Leiden edition's preface gave the line its name, *Textus Receptus*, and the label "today is commonly applied to all editions of the Greek NT before the Elzevir's, beginning with Erasmus' in 1516" (Combs 1996). The full line is on the [[textus-receptus]] page.

**Death.** He died at Basel in 1536. The day and place come from the older encyclopedias only. The 1911 *Britannica*: "Dysentery setting in carried him off on the 12th of July 1536, in his 70th year." The 1909 *Catholic Encyclopedia*: "d. at Basle, Switzerland, 12 July, 1536." No scholarly source for the place and day was found for this page.

## Claims & Disputes

**Contested: the year he was born.** Harry Vredeveld opened a 1993 article in *Renaissance Quarterly* by saying that "neither the wealth of autobiographical information that the Dutch humanist has left us nor the enormous mass of scholarly literature … has ever given us a firm grip on the year of his birth". The two years in play are 1466 and 1469. For 1466, Vredeveld's abstract: "If he were born in 1466 as he indicates at times". For 1469: "R L DeMolen has argued with equal logic that Erasmus must have been born in 1469". (Those words come from the abstract as indexed by OpenAlex, which drops punctuation.) Only the abstract has been read for this page, so Vredeveld's own verdict isn't given here. The older reference works lean to 1466 and say why it's uncertain. The 1911 *Britannica*: "probably in 1466; but his statements about his age are conflicting" and "the year of his birth cannot be definitely fixed". The *Catholic Encyclopedia*: "b. at Rotterdam, Holland, 28 October, probably in 1466". The convention of printing 1466 settles nothing by itself.

**Contested (minor): Gouda or Rotterdam.** He is known as Erasmus of Rotterdam, and the *Catholic Encyclopedia* gives Rotterdam. The 1911 *Britannica*, signed by Mark Pattison and P. S. Allen, reports another account: "A contemporary authority states that he was born at Gouda, his father's native town; but he adopted the style Rotterdammensis or Roterodamus, in accordance with a story to which he himself gave credence."

**Contested: Erasmus's "promise" about the Comma Johanneum.** The story goes that Erasmus left the Comma out of his early editions and promised to put it in if anyone produced a single Greek manuscript that had it. Combs cites Metzger (1992) for that account. Combs sets out the dispute: "it has become the common tradition that Erasmus rashly made a promise to his critics that he would include the Comma if a single Greek manuscript could be brought forward as evidence. However, Henk J. de Jonge has recently demonstrated that nothing in Erasmus' writings indicates he formally made such a promise." De Jonge's argument appeared in *Ephemerides Theologicae Lovanienses* 56 (1980). So the passage entered his third edition in 1522. Whether it went in because of a promise is disputed.

## Where the Muse Leads

- [[textus-receptus]]: the "received text," a name given in 1633 to the Greek line that began with his 1516 edition.
- [[latin-vulgate]]: the Latin Bible he used to fill the gap at the end of Revelation, and the source of Acts 9:6's extra words.
- [[comma-johanneum]]: the verse that entered his third edition, and the promise story that may never have happened.
- [[william-tyndale]]: who translated from Erasmus's 1519 and 1522 Greek.
- [[king-james-bible]]: where this Greek line ended up in English.
