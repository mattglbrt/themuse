William Tyndale's English translation is one of the versions the King James translators were told to use wherever it agreed better with the text than the [[bishops-bible|Bishops' Bible]]. The Muse stops here because of one number. How much of the [[king-james-bible]] is really Tyndale's wording gets quoted everywhere, and the published figures run from 18% to "nine-tenths." This page sets out who measured what, how, and where they said it.

## Historical Context

The translators' rules made the Bishops' Bible the base. Rule 1, in the text Isaac Hall printed in 1881: "The ordinary Bible read in the Church, commonly called the Bishops' Bible, to be followed, and as little altered as the original will permit." Other surviving copies of the rules read "as little altered as the truth of the original will permit" (Blumell & Martin 2019). Rule 14 then named Tyndale's translation first among the ones to check against: "These translations to be used, when they agree better with the text than the Bishops' Bible: Tyndale's, Coverdale's, Matthew's [Rogers'], Whitchurch's [Cranmer's], Geneva." (Hall 1881; the bracketed glosses are in that transcription.)

Tyndale worked from printed Greek. F. H. A. Scrivener, in the preface to his 1881 Greek New Testament, says: "Tyndale himself followed the second and third editions of Erasmus's Greek text (1519, 1522)." That puts him on the same Greek line as the King James New Testament (see [[erasmus]] and [[textus-receptus]]).

## Life & Work

**Birth and Oxford.** Nobody knows when Tyndale was born, and the estimates differ (see Claims & Disputes). The region is agreed. The 1911 *Encyclopaedia Britannica* says he "was born on the Welsh border, probably in Gloucestershire," and Brian Buxton of the Tyndale Society writes: "The Tudor chronicler Edward Hall said that he came from 'about the borders of Wales'".

He studied at Oxford under another surname. The *Dictionary of National Biography* (E. I. Carlyle, 1885-1900): "Tyndale commenced to study at Oxford at the beginning of Easter term 1510 under the name of William Hychyns. According to Foxe, he was entered at Magdalen Hall. He supplicated for admission as B.A. on 13 May 1512, and was admitted on 4 July." He "was licensed for the degree of M.A. on 26 June 1515, and was created M.A. on 2 July". The exact days rest on the DNB alone. The Tyndale Society agrees on the rest: Magdalen Hall, "which later evolved into the present Hertford College," the B.A. in 1512, and the M.A. in 1515.

**Cambridge, perhaps.** What came after Oxford is disputed. The DNB says he "removed to Cambridge, remaining there probably till the close of 1521." Buxton is more cautious: "Again this is possible but seemingly unrecorded in the university archives." The Society's timeline says only: "He may have spent some time at Cambridge University."

**The New Testament, 1525-1526.** The first attempt to print it was at Cologne. Mombert, in the *New Schaff-Herzog Encyclopedia*: "the printing of the New Testament in quarto was begun at Cologne in the summer of 1525". It was stopped. The DNB: "The work had proceeded as far as the sheet bearing the signature K when it was discovered, soon after the beginning of September, by the catholic controversialist John Cochlæus". Cambridge University Library's exhibition *Great and Manifold Blessings* gives what followed: "After an abortive attempt in Cologne a complete edition of this was produced in Worms in 1526." The DNB says "This edition was printed at Worms by Schoeffer in octavo, and illustrated by twelve woodcuts." (The printer's name rests on the DNB alone here.) The Tyndale Society's timeline adds: "It began to be smuggled into England."

**The Pentateuch, 1530, and Jonah, 1531.** Cambridge University Library describes the 1530 volume as "The first printed edition of the Pentateuch (the opening five books of the Old Testament) in English." Its imprint reads "Marlborow: Hans Lufft, 1530", which names Marburg. The Library calls that imprint false: "The first five books of this appeared under a fictitious imprint in 1530. The printer was probably Johannes Hoochstraten of Antwerp". Mombert's article had already doubted it: "it being now an established fact that Hans Luft never had a printing-press at Marburg". Only Genesis carries a date. The DNB: "Genesis bears the date 17 Jan. 1529–30, while the others are undated." So this page doesn't say the Pentateuch was printed at Marburg. The printer is a probability, not a fact.

Jonah came next. The DNB: "Tyndale's translation of the book of Jonah was published with a prologue in 1531, probably from the press of Martin Emperowr at Antwerp." Mombert gives the same order and years: "New Testament, 1525-26; Pentateuch, 1530; Jonah, 1531."

**The 1534 revision.** By 1534 he was living in Antwerp. Buxton: "In the summer of 1534 he took up lodgings in Antwerp with Thomas Poyntz, an English merchant, and his wife Anna." That same summer, Buxton writes, "his former assistant George Joye edited a new edition". Cambridge University Library: "This revised version followed an unauthorised edition with alterations by George Joye, an English scholar living in Antwerp." Tyndale's own revision, *The Newe Testament, dylygently corrected and compared with the Greke by Willyam Tindale*, came out in November 1534 (DNB; Buxton). The imprint on the Bible Society's copy reads "Antwerp: [by] Merten de Keyser, 1534". The DNB names the printer differently: "It was printed in small octavo by Martin Emperowr at Antwerp". None of the sources used here says whether the two names belong to one man, so this page gives both and leaves it there.

**Betrayal, prison, and death.** The 1911 *Britannica*: "In May 1535 he was betrayed by Henry Phillips". The DNB gives a day: "He was arrested on 23 or 24 May 1535". Where he was taken is disputed. The DNB says Phillips "decoyed him from the English House, and betrayed him to the imperial officers." Buxton puts it elsewhere: "In the spring of 1535, Phillips engineered the arrest of Tyndale from Poyntz's house and his incarceration in Vilvoorde Castle near Brussels." Both name Phillips. They disagree on the house.

He was held at Vilvorde. The DNB says he was "conveyed a prisoner to the castle of Vilvorde, the state prison of the Low Countries." The sources agree on how he died. The DNB: "being strangled at the stake and his body afterwards burnt." The Tyndale Society: "he was first strangled and then burned at the stake." Mombert alone places it "in the prison yard". The day is disputed (see below).

**His wording in later Bibles.** The studies below measure his share of the King James New Testament, and of the parts of the Old Testament he translated. Kent P. Jackson puts it "of the sections he translated" (Jackson 2011). His wording also runs through the [[geneva-bible|Geneva Bible]]. Ronald Mansbridge's 1995 word count found "more than 83% of the words in the Geneva Version were taken direct from Tyndale."

## Claims & Disputes

**Contested: when he was born.** The date is unknown, and the ranges on record don't match.
- **Between 1490 and 1495 (DNB; 1911 *Britannica*).** The DNB: "probably between 1490 and 1495." The *Britannica*: "some time between 1490 and 1495."
- **1491-1494 (Tyndale Society).** Buxton: "Tyndale's date of birth is unknown but is likely to have been in the period 1491-1494." The Society's timeline: "His exact date of birth is unknown."
- **1484 (Mombert).** The *New Schaff-Herzog* article gives a single year and a village: "b. most probably at North Nibley (15 m. s.s.w. of Gloucester), England, in 1484". No other source used here gives 1484.

**Contested: the day he died.** The DNB says "Tyndale was executed at Vilvorde on 6 Oct. 1536". The 1911 *Britannica*: "On the 6th of October 1536 he was strangled at the stake". Mombert gives "Oct. 6, 1536." The Tyndale Society is less precise. Its timeline: "In late September or early October, 1536, he was first strangled and then burned at the stake." Buxton: "strangled and burned, in the autumn of 1536." The three that give 6 October are all older reference works, and the research for this page didn't find the primary record behind that day. So this page gives the year, 1536, and leaves the day open.

**Contested: how much of the King James Bible is Tyndale's.** The published estimates disagree, and they were reached by different methods. Here they are with who made each one.

- **About 84% of the New Testament and about 76% of the Old (Nielson & Skousen, 1998).** Jon Nielson and Royal Skousen, in the journal *Reformation* 3 (1998), sampled the text. Their abstract: "Based on 18 sampled passages ... for the New Testament Tyndale's contribution is about 84 per cent of the text, while in the Old Testament about 76 per cent of his words have been retained." Only the abstract has been read for this page, so how they counted "retained" words isn't given here. Two later works report the same figures: David Rolph Seely (2004), "about 76 percent of the Old Testament and 84 percent of the New Testament text of the King James Version are the retained words of Tyndale," and Jonathan D. Moore in a Trinitarian Bible Society lecture, "about 84% of the AV New Testament and about 76% of the Old Testament is verbatim Tyndale."
- **The range of earlier estimates, as Nielson & Skousen give it.** Their abstract says earlier estimates "have run from a high of up to 90 per cent (Westcott) to a low of 18 per cent (Butterworth)." Both attributions are theirs. Moore adds that "Butterworth's calculations that gave him a figure of only 18% were deeply flawed methodologically, as Nielson and Skousen have shown," citing Charles C. Butterworth, *The Literary Lineage of the King James Bible* (1941).
- **83% (Daniell, 1997).** On 4 June 1997, opening his "Let There Be Light" exhibition at the Library of Congress, David Daniell said that "83 percent of the King James New Testament is Tyndale exactly." The Library's *Information Bulletin* reported: "He attributed the figure to a student at Brigham Young University who compared the texts using a computer." Daniell had learned of it that January, "when he visited the college in Provo, Utah" (French 1997).
- **Over 80% and over 75% (Jackson, 2011).** A later BYU chapter says: "Research has shown that over 75 percent of the King James Old Testament (of the sections he translated) comes from Tyndale as well as over 80 percent of the King James New Testament." Its footnote cites John Nielson's 1994 BYU master's thesis, "Authorship of the King James Version of the Bible."
- **More than 81% (Mansbridge, 1995).** Ronald Mansbridge, in the *Tyndale Society Journal* no. 3, counted words in nine chapters: Genesis 13, Deuteronomy 8, 1 Samuel 8, Matthew 3, Acts 25:1-15, Romans 13, 1 Corinthians 5, James 4, and Revelation 20. In his words: "I followed the simple procedure of counting the total number of words in nine representative chapters." He found "more than 81% of the words in the King James Version" came from Tyndale. He also noted that earlier figures "varied, ranging from two-thirds to 90%," and reported that Daniell had put the New Testament share at nine-tenths.
- **"Nine-tenths" (Mombert).** J. I. Mombert's article on Tyndale in the *New Schaff-Herzog Encyclopedia of Religious Knowledge*, vol. XII, says his work "constitutes nine-tenths of that translation, and very largely that of the Revised Version." No source or measurement is given for the figure.
- **55% (Smith, 2019).** Joul Layne Smith's PhD dissertation at the University of Texas at Arlington concludes: "The KJV, though often considered to have borrowed 85% of Tyndale, based on this study, only borrowed 55% of Tyndale's Bible." The abstract doesn't describe the method, and the full text hasn't been read for this page.

So the figure is disputed. The 84/76 figure has the most behind it: a peer-reviewed article, reported by two other writers. A word count of nine chapters, a sample of 18 passages, and a round number printed without a source don't measure the same thing, and they haven't produced the same answer.

## Where the Muse Leads

- [[king-james-bible]]: the Bible whose wording is being measured.
- [[bishops-bible]]: the official base text, which Tyndale's version was to correct where it read better.
- [[geneva-bible]]: listed beside Tyndale in Rule 14, and by Mansbridge's count mostly Tyndale's words too.
- [[erasmus]]: Scrivener says Tyndale translated from Erasmus's 1519 and 1522 Greek editions.
- [[textus-receptus]]: the later name for the Greek text line Tyndale and the King James translators both stood on.
