The *Textus Receptus*, the "received text," is a name for the printed Greek New Testament in the editions of Erasmus, Stephanus, Beza, and the Elzevirs. The King James New Testament was translated from this line. The Muse stops here because the name sounds like an ancient pedigree, and it isn't one. It comes from a sentence in the preface of a Leiden edition of 1633, and was applied after the fact to editions going back to Erasmus in 1516.

## Historical Context

The line starts in Basel. [[erasmus|Erasmus]]'s Greek New Testament was printed there by Johann Froben and published in 1516 as a Latin-Greek edition, the *Novum Instrumentum*. The Complutensian Polyglot had been printed in 1514, but Erasmus's was "the first one to be published" (Combs 1996). He built it from seven manuscripts in Basel, and for Revelation he had only one (Combs 1996, pp. 45-46).

The King James translators' own preface says they worked from the Greek for the New Testament: "If you aske what they had before them, truely it was the Hebrew text of the Olde Testament, the Greeke of the New." (*The Translators to the Reader*, 1611, Wikisource transcription.)

## The Document

**Stephanus.** Stephanus (Estienne) printed four editions, in 1546, 1549, 1550, and 1551. Combs: "His third edition of 1550 was the first to have a critical apparatus ... It was republished many times and became the accepted form of the TR, especially in England." In the 1551 edition "the text is divided into numbered verses for the first time" (Combs 1996, p. 52).

**Beza.** "Theodore Beza, the successor of John Calvin at Geneva, produced nine editions between 1565 and 1604. Only four are independent editions, the others being smaller-sized reprints. His text was essentially a reprinting of Stephanus with minor changes." (Combs 1996, pp. 52-53.)

**What the King James translators used.** F. H. A. Scrivener, preparing his 1881 Greek text of the Authorised Version, reasoned: "Between 1598 and 1611 no important edition appeared; so that Beza's fifth and last text of 1598 was more likely than any other to be in the hands of King James's revisers." He took Beza 1598 as his base and listed where the King James Version departs from it: "All variations from Beza's text of 1598, in number about 190, are set down in an appendix at the end of the volume." Blumell (2019): "The translators of the King James Bible made extensive use of Beza's 1588–89 and 1598 editions." Both Blumell and Combs cite Scrivener's preface for this, so the Beza 1598 identification rests on Scrivener's collation.

Timothy Berg, writing on the translators' annotated 1602 Bishops' Bible, names "the editions of the Greek New Testament they had available from Erasmus (1535), Stephanus (1550), and Beza (1598)" (Berg 2023).

**The name.** The label comes from the Elzevirs' second edition, printed at Leiden in 1633. Its preface has this sentence: "Textum ergo habes, nunc ab omnibus receptum, in quo nihil immutatum aut corruptum damus." Combs notes the phrase "today is commonly applied to all editions of the Greek NT before the Elzevir's, beginning with Erasmus' in 1516." Citing de Jonge, he adds that the edition was edited by Jeremias Hoelzlin, that the preface was by Daniel Heinsius, and that Abraham Elzevir was Bonaventura's nephew, not his brother (Combs 1996, p. 35 and nn. 1-2). Blumell says the text "came to be regarded for many centuries as the Textus Receptus."

## Contents

Combs gives two readings in this Greek line that came from the [[latin-vulgate|Latin Vulgate]] rather than from Greek manuscripts, and dates the entry of one disputed verse.

- **Revelation 22:16-21.** Erasmus's only manuscript of Revelation "was missing its last page and thus the last six verses of Revelation (22:16–21)," so he "retranslated these verses from the Latin Vulgate, and he honestly admitted in the Annotationes that he had done so" (Combs 1996, p. 47).
- **Acts 9:6.** The words "And he trembling and astonished said, Lord, what wilt thou have me to do? And the Lord said unto him" "were inserted by Erasmus at this point because they were in the Vulgate" (Combs 1996, p. 47). Combs says they have no Greek manuscript support and stayed in the received text.
- **1 John 5:7b-8a.** Not in the first edition. The [[comma-johanneum|Comma Johanneum]] first appeared in Erasmus's third edition, of 1522 (Combs 1996, pp. 48-50).

Scrivener also found places where the King James Version follows the Latin rather than any Greek: "in some places the Authorised version corresponds but loosely with any form of the Greek original, while it exactly follows the Latin Vulgate."

## Claims & Disputes

- **Established:** the name is a later label. It comes from the 1633 Elzevir preface and was applied back to the earlier editions (Combs 1996; Blumell 2019).
- **Contested:** the story that Erasmus promised to add the Comma Johanneum if one Greek manuscript turned up. Combs cites Metzger (1992) for the promise and reports that Henk J. de Jonge (1980) "has recently demonstrated that nothing in Erasmus' writings indicates he formally made such a promise." See [[comma-johanneum]].
