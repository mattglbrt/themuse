The Hermetica are a body of theological and philosophical writings from late antiquity that were long believed to be much older, the work of Hermes Trismegistus, who was thought to be a contemporary of Moses. [[marsilio-ficino]]'s Latin translation, the *Pimander*, was printed in 1471. The Muse stops here because these are the texts behind the word "Hermetic", and because what they are, and how much they mattered, is still argued over. This page covers only what's been verified so far; the texts themselves get their own trail.

## Historical Context

Brian Copenhaver, introducing his modern English translation, describes them this way: "The Hermetica are a body of theological-philosophical texts written in late antiquity, but long believed to be much older. Their supposed author, Hermes Trismegistus, was thought to be a contemporary of Moses" (Copenhaver, *Hermetica*, 1992, publisher's description). That's the whole story in two sentences: late texts, taken for ancient ones.

## The Document

**Ficino's Latin.** Ficino finished his translation in 1463, according to Wouter Hanegraaff, who calls it "his famous translation, finished in 1463" (Hanegraaff, blog, 2013). It was printed at Treviso on 18 December 1471. The standard catalogue of fifteenth-century printed books records it as "Pimander seu De potestate et sapientia Dei. Aus dem Griech. übers. von Marsilius Ficinus. Hrsg.(?) Franciscus Rolandellus. Treviso: Gerardus de Lisa, 18.XII.1471. 8°" (*Gesamtkatalog der Wiegendrucke*, GW 12310). The catalogue line says it was translated from the Greek. Hanegraaff describes the same edition as "first published in Treviso in 1471 as Hermes Trismegistus' book on the Power and Wisdom of God".

**Hanegraaff's question.** The title of Hanegraaff's 2013 post is "Butchering the Corpus Hermeticum: Breaking News on Ficino's Pimander", and he asks whether Ficino's translation "had in fact been so bad as the edition would seem to suggest?" What he concludes isn't covered here.

**Casaubon, 1614.** Walter Scott, editing the texts in 1924, wrote that they were taken at face value "for about thirteen hundred years, from the time of Lactantius to that of Casaubon" (Scott, *Hermetica* I, 1924, Introduction). The Casaubon in question is [[isaac-casaubon]], whose *De rebus sacris et ecclesiasticis exercitationes XVI* (London, 1614) took the texts apart in Exercitatio I, section X, pp. 70–87, as part of his answer to Cardinal Baronius. His verdict comes on p. 75, where he calls the book circulating under Mercurius Trismegistus's name pseudepigraphal, "Christiani alicuius, vel vt dicam melius, semichristiani merum figmentum". The section closes on p. 87: "Falsissimum etiam est quod docti indocti videntur hactenus credidisse; aut scripta hæc fuisse à Mercurio Trismegisto, vetustissimo Ægypti sapiente; aut ex illius scriptis esse versa. quorum vtrumque probauimus esse longe absurdissimum."

In a working translation made for this research, not yet checked by a Latinist, those read: "the mere fiction of some Christian, or, to put it better, of a half-Christian", and "Most false too is what learned and unlearned alike seem to have believed until now: either that these writings were by Mercurius Trismegistus, the most ancient sage of Egypt, or that they were translated from his writings. We have proved both of these to be by far the most absurd." No published English translation of these passages turned up. The Latin is as transcribed from the 1614 page images, long s printed as s.

His grounds, summarised from the pages read (all English here is paraphrase of the Latin):

- *Content.* The doctrine is partly Greek, from Plato and the Platonists, "& quidem persæpe ipsis eorũ verbis", often in their very words, and partly Christian, from Scripture (p. 77). He reads Poimandres as "Pastor", the shepherd, and says "Pœmander hic siue Pastor, Christus est, qui apud Iohannem ait", going on to quote John's "I am the good shepherd" (p. 77).
- *Anachronism.* The texts mention the sculptor Phidias and a victory by Eunomus of Locri at the Pythian games, which Casaubon says were founded many centuries after the Egyptian Hermes (p. 86; summary on p. 71).
- *Language.* The style is foreign to the Greek of Hermes's supposed contemporaries, some words were unknown even to the Greek of the time of Christ's birth, and the texts were written in Greek, not translated from Egyptian (p. 86).
- *The general case.* Christian mysteries hidden before Christ are stated plainly here, the old Greeks knew nothing of them, and the early Church had a custom of forging books "bono fine", for a good end (p. 70, section summary).

He dates the forger to "primis illis seculis nascentis Christianismi" (p. 77), those first centuries of nascent Christianity, and gives no year in the pages read.

## Claims & Disputes

**Established in outline, contested in detail: when were they written?** Modern scholarship dates the texts after Christ, as Casaubon did. Anna Van den Kerchove describes the Corpus Hermeticum as gathering "17 textes grecs, écrits au cours du iie et du iiie siècle", seventeen Greek texts written in the second and third centuries AD, and dates the Poimandres, one of the oldest, between the late first and mid-second century (*Revue de l'histoire des religions*, 2014). Scott in 1924 put most or all of them in the third century: "Some of them may have been written before the end of the second century; but probably none so early as the first century." So the two differ on the Poimandres. Paolo Scarpi says their dating "demeure encore problématique et très difficile à déterminer", is still problematic and very hard to pin down (*Annuaire de l'EPHE*, 2012).

**Established: Casaubon wasn't the first to doubt them.** Florian Audureau says Casaubon proved in 1614 that these were late texts, formerly taken for the ancient wisdom of the *prisca theologia*, and that he did it "À la suite de plusieurs philologues du xvie siècle", following several sixteenth-century philologists, whom that sentence doesn't name (*Revue de l'histoire des religions*, 2019). Scarpi says the polemic between Casaubon and Baronius dealt Hermetism "un coup très grave", a very serious blow. Scott's "thirteen hundred years ... to that of Casaubon" should be read with Audureau's qualification in mind.

**Unresolved on this page: who wrote them?** Casaubon's answer was a Christian, or half-Christian, working from Plato and Scripture. Van den Kerchove describes the texts as offering "une voie de sagesse mêlant traditions grecque et égyptienne", a path of wisdom mixing Greek and Egyptian traditions, and she notes that Casaubon's shepherd reading "est lié à son idée que les textes hermétiques ont été écrits par des chrétiens", is tied to his idea that Christians wrote them. None of the sources checked for this page rules on Casaubon's Christian-authorship claim, so the page leaves it open.

**Contested: are the Hermetica magical texts?** The popular picture ties Renaissance magic to these writings. Copenhaver disagrees about the ones Ficino translated: "But those Hermetic writings, the ones that Ficino translated, aren't about magic, and Ficino understood that. He says so in his writings" (interview, 2021). That's one leading specialist, speaking informally.

**Contested: a "Hermetic Tradition"?** Hanegraaff argues against the belief, which he traces mainly to Kristeller (1938) and Frances Yates (1964), that an important "Hermetic Tradition" began with Ficino's translation. In his words, "it has been widely assumed that an important 'Hermetic Tradition' emerged during the Renaissance" and "This article argues that these assumptions need to be revised." His conclusion: "we seem to be dealing with a discourse about Hermes, but hardly a 'tradition'" (*Aries*, 2015, abstract). The quoted abstract was rebuilt from an index and should be checked against the published article before an episode uses it word for word. See [[hermeticism]] and [[yates-thesis]].

## Where the Muse Leads

- [[marsilio-ficino]]: his Latin translation, finished in 1463 and printed at Treviso in 1471.
- [[hermeticism]]: the name for everything later built on them, and a name historians now argue about.
- [[yates-thesis]]: Hanegraaff traces the idea of a Hermetic Tradition starting with these texts mainly to Kristeller and Yates.
- [[isaac-casaubon]]: in 1614 he argued, from Plato, Scripture, anachronism, and language, that these texts were neither Egyptian nor ancient.
