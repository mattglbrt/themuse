*Daemonologie* is King James VI of Scotland's book on magic, witchcraft, and spirits, a dialogue in three books printed by Robert Waldegrave in 1597. The Muse stops here because the king whose name is on the [[king-james-bible]] wrote a treatise to prove that witchcraft is real and that witches should be punished severely, a few years after he had sat in on the examinations of people accused in the [[north-berwick-witch-trials|North Berwick witch hunt]].

## Historical Context

In 1590 and 1591 Scotland went through the witch hunt now named for North Berwick, and the king was in the middle of it. The standard scholarly edition of the documents describes it as "the North Berwick witch hunt — in which King James was involved as alleged victim, interrogator, judge, and demonologist" (Normand & Roberts 2000, abstract). The University of Edinburgh's *Survey of Scottish Witchcraft* lists "James (VI) Stewart" among the investigators in the case of [[agnes-sampson|Agnes Sampson]], and records that "She confessed to these things in front of King James VI" (Survey, case C/EGD/63). The London pamphlet [[newes-from-scotland|Newes from Scotland]] has her "brought to Haliriud house before the Kings Maiestie," where "she was straitly examined."

*Daemonologie* came out in 1597. When James actually wrote it is not settled on this page: the scholarly discussion of its composition date sits in a chapter of Normand & Roberts that hasn't been checked yet.

## The Document

The first edition's title page, as transcribed by Project Gutenberg, reads: "Daemonologie / In Forme of a Dialogie / Diuided into three Bookes. / By James RX / Printed by Robert Walde-graue, / Printer to the Kings Majestie. An. 1597. / Cum Privilegio Regio." (slashes mark line breaks; the spellings are as transcribed). The title page gives no place. Library catalogues supply Edinburgh: the University of Wisconsin-Madison record reads "Edinburgh : Printed by Robert Walde-grave, Printer to the Kings Majestie, an. 1597."

London printers reissued it in 1603. One edition was "printed for William Aspley and W. Cotton, according to the copie printed at Edenburgh" (STC 14365, per its Open Library record), which also backs Edinburgh as the place of the first printing. A second came from "London : Arnold Hatfield for Robert VVald-graue, 1603" (STC 14366, per the Wellcome Collection catalogue). A microfilm scan of a 1603 copy is free on the Internet Archive.

Two modern editions matter. G. B. Harrison reprinted it in 1924 in the Bodley Head Quartos, from the Bodleian copy Douce I. 2305. His note says "The text (not the Preface) is reprinted line for line and page for page," so his preface does not follow the 1597 layout exactly, and this page quotes the preface from the Gutenberg transcription instead. Lawrence Normand and Gareth Roberts's *Witchcraft in Early Modern Scotland* (University of Exeter Press, 2000) prints the full texts of *Demonology* and *News from Scotland* together, and it is the standard scholarly edition.

## Contents

James lays out the plan in his preface: "I haue put it in forme of a Dialogue, which I haue diuided into three bookes: The first speaking of Magie in general, and Necromancie in special. The second of Sorcerie and Witch-craft: and the thirde, conteines a discourse of all these kindes of spirits, & Spectres" (1597 text, Gutenberg #25929, preface). The two speakers are introduced with the line "PHILOMATHES and EPISTEMON reason the matter."

The preface opens with his reason for writing:

> "The fearefull aboundinge at this time in this countrie, of these detestable slaues of the Deuill, the Witches or enchaunters, hath moved me (beloued reader) to dispatch in post, this following treatise of mine"

He says the book is "not in any wise (as I protest) to serue for a shew of my learning & ingine, but onely (mooued of conscience) to preasse thereby, so farre as I can, to resolue the doubting harts of many" ... "both that such assaultes of Sathan are most certainly practized, & that the instrumentes thereof, merits most severly to be punished" (Gutenberg #25929, preface).

He then names two writers he is arguing against. The first is [[reginald-scot|Reginald Scot]]: "the one called SCOT an Englishman, is not ashamed in publike print to deny, that ther can be such a thing as Witch-craft." The second is [[johann-weyer|Johann Weyer]]: "The other called VVIERVS, a German Phisition, sets out a publick apologie for al these craftes-folkes" (Gutenberg #25929, preface).

## Claims & Disputes

- **Established:** James took a personal part in the North Berwick hunt as interrogator and judge, and at least one of the accused confessed in front of him (Normand & Roberts 2000; Survey, case C/EGD/63).
- **Not yet checked:** whether the preface itself mentions North Berwick or the 1590-91 trials. The passages quoted above give a general motive, witches "aboundinge at this time in this countrie." This page does not claim the preface names the trials.

## Where the Muse Leads

- [[king-james-bible]]: the king who put his name on the Bible had examined accused witches himself, and wrote this book to argue they should be punished.
- [[james-i]]: the author, and the king in the room when the accused were examined.
- [[north-berwick-witch-trials]]: the hunt James sat in on, and the obvious background to a king's book about witches.
- [[newes-from-scotland]]: the London pamphlet that reported the king's examinations, printed with *Demonology* in the standard modern edition.
- [[reginald-scot]]: the English sceptic James attacks by name in the preface.
- [[johann-weyer]]: the German physician James accuses of defending witches.
- [[john-dee]]: in 1604 Dee petitioned James to be cleared of "the horrible slander" that he was a "conjurer, or caller, or invocator of divels" (DNB). No answer from the king is recorded. The DNB's report that he refused rests on Thomas Smith's hedged Latin life of Dee (1707) and is contested.
